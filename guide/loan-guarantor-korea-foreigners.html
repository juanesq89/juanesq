Guaranteeing Someone’s Loan in Korea (보증·연대보증): What You Owe and How to Push Back

By Juan, Attorney at Law 8 min read English · Español · Português

A friend, a colleague or your partner asks you to “just sign” as guarantor for a loan, a debt or a business deal. In Korea that signature can make the whole debt yours. Korean law also sets strict conditions on how a guarantee is made — and a guarantee that misses them may not bind you at all.

This guide covers guarantees of money debts under the Civil Act (민법) and the Act on Special Cases Concerning the Protection of Guarantors (보증인 보호를 위한 특별법). An identity guarantee (신원보증) given for someone’s visa or stay is a different system and is not covered here. If you lent the money yourself and want it back, see recovering a personal loan.

→What a Guarantor Takes On

A guarantor must perform the obligation the principal debtor fails to perform (Civil Act Art. 428(1)). The guarantee covers more than the principal: it includes interest, penalties, damages and other obligations attached to the main debt (Art. 429(1)). It can also be given for future debts (Art. 428(2)). What it cannot do is exceed the main debt — if the guarantor’s burden is heavier in content or form, it is reduced to the main debt (Art. 430).

→No Signature on Paper, No Guarantee

This is the rule foreign residents most often do not know:

A guarantee takes effect only if the intention to guarantee is expressed in writing bearing the guarantor’s name and seal or signature. A guarantee expressed in electronic form has no effect.

That is Article 428-2(1). A “yes, I’ll guarantee it” in a chat message or an e-mail is not a valid guarantee under this rule. The same written form applies when a guarantee is later changed to the guarantor’s disadvantage (Art. 428-2(2)). One trap: if you pay on the guarantee, you can no longer use the missing form to call it void, to the extent of what you paid (Art. 428-2(3)).

A guarantee for an open-ended series of debts (근보증) — for example everything a business owes a supplier — is void unless the maximum amount is specified in that written document (Art. 428-3).

→Ordinary Guarantee or Joint Guarantee (연대보증)

Ordinary guarantee (보증)Joint guarantee (연대보증)
“Ask the debtor first”Available: if you prove the debtor can pay and enforcement is easy, you can demand the creditor go after the debtor first (Art. 437)Not available (Art. 437, proviso)
Several guarantorsEach owes an equal share unless agreed otherwise (Arts. 439, 408)Each can be asked for the whole amount
Debtor’s defencesYou can raise the debtor’s defences against the creditor, and the debtor giving them up does not bind you (Art. 433)

Most loan forms in Korea use 연대보증. If the document says 연대, assume the creditor can come straight to you.

→What the Creditor Has to Tell You

Under Article 436-2 of the Civil Act the creditor must:

  • tell you, when the guarantee is made or renewed, about the debtor’s credit information it holds or knows that could affect your decision;
  • tell you without delay if the debtor fails to pay principal, interest or other attached debts for three months or more, if it learns in advance that the debtor cannot pay on time, or if the debtor’s credit information changes significantly;
  • on your request, tell you what the main debt is and whether it is being paid.

If the creditor breaks these duties and you suffer loss, the court may reduce or cancel the guarantee (Art. 436-2(4)).

→Extra Protection When You Guaranteed as a Favour

The Guarantor Protection Act aims at guarantees given as a favour, without anything in return (Art. 1). It does not protect, among others, business owners and directors guaranteeing their own company’s debts or business partners guaranteeing partnership debts (Art. 2, item 1). If you are covered:

  • Maximum amount in writing — the guarantee’s maximum must be specified in writing, also on renewal (Art. 4).
  • Notice of default — the creditor must tell you without delay of a default of three months or more; a financial institution must tell you after one month (Art. 5(1)–(2)). If it does not, you are released to the extent of the loss this caused you (Art. 5(4)).
  • Three-year default period — if no guarantee period was agreed, it is deemed three years (Art. 7(1)). The Supreme Court reads this as the period in which the guaranteed debts arise, not a date on which your liability simply expires (2018다42231, 2020-07-23).
  • Banks must show you the debtor’s credit information and get your signature on it (Art. 8(1)). If they did not, you can ask for it; if they do not respond within 7 days, you can give notice to terminate within one month of learning that, effective one month after the bank receives it (Art. 8(3)–(4)).
  • Terms that are worse for you than the Act are void (Art. 11).

→After You Pay: Getting It Back From the Debtor

A guarantor who signed at the debtor’s request and pays without fault has a right of reimbursement (구상권) against the debtor (Art. 441). In some situations you can claim it before paying — for example when the debt has fallen due, or when a court has ordered you to pay (Art. 442). If you guaranteed without the debtor asking, you recover only what the debtor gained at the time (Art. 444). Between several guarantors, one who pays more than their share can recover the excess from the others (Art. 448).

In practice the debtor who could not pay the bank often cannot pay you either. Recovery then runs through the same tools as any debt — a payment order or a lawsuit, then enforcement.

→What to Do Now

  1. Before signing, read whether it says 보증 or 연대보증, the maximum amount and the period — and assume you will pay it.
  2. If a creditor demands payment, ask in writing for a copy of the document bearing your signature or seal.
  3. Check the form: no paper signature, no maximum amount for an open-ended guarantee — these can end the claim.
  4. Do not pay part “to calm things down” before checking — payment can cure a defect in form.
  5. Ask when the debtor stopped paying and when you were told; late notice can reduce what you owe.
  6. Keep every receipt of what you pay; it is the basis of your claim against the debtor.
