Littering, Public Urination, a Drunken Scene: Korea's Minor Offenses Act (경범죄) and the Ticket That Follows

By Juan, Attorney at Law 6 min read English · Español · Português

A police officer stops you for dropping a cigarette butt, for relieving yourself in an alley after a night out, or for shouting in a restaurant, and hands you a slip of paper. Most foreigners pay it, or throw it away, without knowing what it is. It is a notice under the Minor Offenses Act (경범죄 처벌법), and what happens next depends on what you do within ten days.

The Minor Offenses Act lists everyday misconduct that is punishable but not serious enough for the Criminal Act. The penalties are small. The procedure is what catches people out: an unpaid ticket does not simply grow into a bigger fine, it becomes a request to a judge for a summary trial.

→What Counts as a Minor Offense

Article 3 sets three levels. The items below are the ones foreign residents and visitors most often meet.

A court can waive the penalty or combine detention and a petty fine, depending on the circumstances (Art. 5).

→The Ticket: Ten Days, Then Twenty

For most Article 3(1) and 3(2) conduct the police chief can, instead of sending the case to court, issue a written notice ordering you to pay a set amount (범칙금, beomchikgeum) (Arts. 6 and 7). The amount for each type of conduct is fixed by Presidential Decree.

  1. Pay within 10 days of receiving the notice, at a designated bank or post office (Art. 8(1)), or by card through an authorised payment agency (Art. 8-2).
  2. If you miss that, you have 20 more days to pay the amount plus 20% (Art. 8(2)).
  3. If you miss both, the police chief must ask a court for a summary trial (즉결심판) (Art. 9(1)). Until that request is made, you can still end the matter by paying the amount plus 50%. After the request, paying the amount plus 50% and submitting proof before the judgment also leads the police to withdraw it (Art. 9(2)).

Once you have paid, you cannot be punished again for the same act (Arts. 8(3) and 9(3)).

Paying the ticket ends it. Ignoring the ticket turns it into a court case.

→When There Is No Ticket

The ticket route is not available to everyone. It does not apply to a person who commits the conduct habitually, where detention is considered the appropriate response, where the conduct has a victim, or to anyone under 18 (Art. 6(2)). And the police will not issue a ticket to someone who refuses to accept it, or whose address or identity is unclear (Art. 7(1)). Those cases go to a summary trial directly.

For a foreigner, the second point is practical. Foreigners aged 17 or over staying in Korea must carry their passport or alien registration card (including the mobile version) and show it when an official asks in the course of their duties (Immigration Act Art. 27). Not doing so is a separate offence, punishable by a fine of up to 1 million won (Art. 98(i)).

→The Summary Trial

In a summary trial, a judge of a district court, branch court or municipal court can impose a fine of up to 200,000 won, short detention (구류) or a petty fine (과료) (Act on Summary Trial Procedures Art. 2). When the sentence is a fine or a petty fine, the judgment can be given without you attending (Art. 8-2). Unlike the ticket, this is a criminal sentence.

If you disagree, you can ask for a formal trial within 7 days of the judgment being pronounced or notified, by filing the request with the police chief (Art. 14(1)). The summary judgment then loses effect once the formal trial gives its judgment (Art. 15). This is different from a summary order (약식명령) issued on the prosecutor's request; see summary orders in Korea.

→When It Is More Than a Minor Offense

  • A drunken scene that stops a bar or shop from operating can be treated as obstruction of business under the Criminal Act. See obstruction of business (업무방해).
  • Pushing or hitting an officer at the police station moves from Article 3(3)(i) to obstruction of official duties (공무집행방해).
  • Following, waiting for or repeatedly contacting one person against their wishes is also covered by Article 3(1)(xli), but it can fall under the stalking law instead. See stalking and restraining orders.
  • Entering a home or a managed building rather than an empty one is trespassing under the Criminal Act. See trespassing in Korea.
  • Leaving without paying when you never intended to pay can be investigated as fraud rather than a minor offense. See fraud charges in Korea.

If an officer is deciding between a ticket and something more, stay calm, show your ID, and do not argue the facts on the street. Your account belongs in the interview, and your rights there are in questioned by Korean police.
