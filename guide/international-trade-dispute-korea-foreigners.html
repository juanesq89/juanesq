Trade Disputes With a Korean Company (무역분쟁): Unpaid Goods, Bad Shipments and Where to Fight

By Juan, Attorney at Law 9 min read English · Español · Português

The goods arrived damaged, or they arrived fine and the invoice was never paid. Your partner is in Korea, the contract was in English, and the question now is not who is right — it is where, under which law, and how fast you can act before the money moves.

This guide is for businesses and individual traders abroad whose dispute is with a Korean company: a buyer that stopped paying, a supplier that shipped the wrong goods, a distributor or supply relationship that broke down. It is not about consumer purchases or small personal debts — those have their own routes, such as the small claims procedure. A cross-border sale between two businesses raises three questions a domestic dispute does not: which forum, which law, and whether the result can be enforced where the assets are.

→Start With the Dispute Clause

Before anything else, find the clause on disputes in the contract, the purchase order terms, the distribution agreement or the general terms printed on the back of the pro forma invoice. It usually decides the first two questions for you.

  • An arbitration clause — for example naming the Korean Commercial Arbitration Board (대한상사중재원, KCAB) or another institution. Under Article 8 of the Korean Arbitration Act (중재법) an arbitration agreement must be in writing, but an exchange of emails or other electronic messages that records it counts as writing. If one side then sues in a Korean court anyway, the other side can object, and the court dismisses the case (Article 9).
  • A court clause — choosing the courts of Korea or of another country. Article 8 of the Private International Law Act (국제사법) allows parties to agree on international jurisdiction, subject to the exceptions it lists.
  • No clause at all — then a Korean court has jurisdiction where the parties or the dispute have a substantial connection with Korea (Private International Law Act, Article 2). A Korean defendant with its business in Korea is normally enough. Arbitration, by contrast, is never available without an agreement — both sides would have to sign one now.

Arbitration exists only if both sides agreed to it. A court is there whether anyone agreed or not.

→Which Law Applies: the CISG

Korea acceded to the UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG, 국제물품매매계약에 관한 국제연합 협약) in February 2004, and it has been in force for Korea since 1 March 2005, according to the UNCITRAL status table. Under Article 1(1) of the Convention, it applies to a sale of goods between parties whose places of business are in different States when both States are contracting States, or when the conflict-of-laws rules lead to the law of a contracting State.

  • Many trading partners are parties — the United States, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, Spain, Portugal, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Peru and Australia, among others, appear in the UNCITRAL status table
  • Some are not — the United Kingdom, Ireland, India and South Africa do not appear. With a seller or buyer there, the Convention can still apply through Article 1(1)(b) if the governing law turns out to be Korean
  • The parties can opt out — Article 6 lets them exclude the Convention. A clause saying only "governed by the laws of Korea" is not necessarily an opt-out, because the CISG is part of Korean law for international sales; an exclusion has to be clear
  • No choice of law in the contract — under Articles 45 and 46 of the Private International Law Act, the contract follows the law the parties chose expressly or impliedly; if none, the law of the country most closely connected. For a contract made in the course of business, that is presumed to be the country where the party performing the characteristic obligation — in a sale, usually the seller — has its place of business

The CISG governs the contract itself: delivery, conformity of the goods, payment, remedies, interest. It does not set limitation periods, so those come from the national law that applies.

→Defective or Short Shipment: the Notice Trap

Buyers lose more claims here than anywhere else, and not on the merits. Under the CISG the buyer must examine the goods "within as short a period as is practicable" (Article 38(1)), and loses the right to rely on a lack of conformity unless it gives the seller notice specifying the nature of the problem within a reasonable time after discovering it or when it ought to have discovered it (Article 39(1)). In any event the right is lost if no notice is given within two years of the date the goods were actually handed over, unless a contractual guarantee period says otherwise (Article 39(2)).

If the CISG has been excluded and Korean law governs a sale between merchants, the rule is stricter. Article 69 of the Commercial Act (상법) requires the buyer to inspect the goods without delay on receipt and to send notice immediately on finding a defect or shortage — otherwise it cannot rescind, demand a price reduction or claim damages. For a defect that could not be found at once, the same applies if the buyer discovers it within six months. The rule does not protect a seller who acted in bad faith.

In practice: photograph the goods and packaging on arrival, get an independent inspection or survey report where the value justifies it, and send a written notice that names the defect — "goods not acceptable" is not enough.

→Unpaid Invoice: Interest and the Clock

A seller owed the price is entitled to interest on it under Article 78 of the CISG, in addition to any damages; the Convention does not fix the rate. The more urgent point is time. If Korean law governs the limitation question, the price of goods sold by a merchant or producer becomes time-barred after three years of not being claimed (Civil Act, Article 163, item 6) — shorter than many sellers assume. How limitation is interrupted, and why a demand letter alone does not stop it for long, is covered in the guide on limitation periods in Korea.

→The Demand Letter and the Negotiation

Most trade disputes still end in a negotiated payment, a discount or a replacement shipment. A formal demand letter sent in Korean — by certified content mail (내용증명), so there is proof of what was sent and when — changes the tone of that negotiation. It should state the contract, the shipment or invoice, the breach, the amount and a deadline, and it should say which forum you will use if there is no answer.

Two cautions. Do not send the letter before you have decided whether to freeze assets: it is also a warning to the other side. And keep every settlement offer in writing, with the agreed amount, the payment dates and what happens if a payment is missed.

→KCAB Arbitration or a Korean Court

KCAB, based in Seoul, handles international cases through its international arbitration center (KCAB International) under its own international arbitration rules. When the contract leaves you a choice — or when both sides are willing to agree on arbitration after the dispute arises — the differences look like this:

PointArbitration (e.g. KCAB)Korean court
Who decidesArbitrators chosen under the agreement and the institution's rulesJudges assigned by the court
LanguageAgreed by the parties; failing agreement, set by the tribunal — and Korean only if neither does (Arbitration Act, Article 23)Proceedings are in Korean; foreign-language documents need translation
FinalityThe award has the same effect as a final court judgment (Article 35). It can be set aside only on limited grounds, by an action filed within three months of receiving the award (Article 36(3))Appeals are possible, so a case can pass through more than one level
PrivacyGenerally private; hearings are not open to the publicHearings are in principle public
Enforcement abroadUnder the New York Convention in its contracting StatesDepends on each country's rules for recognising foreign judgments

Neither route is automatically cheaper or faster. Arbitration costs include the institution's fees and the arbitrators' fees; litigation costs include court fees, translation and the time of more than one instance. The right choice depends on the amount, where the other side's assets are, and whether you will need to enforce outside Korea.

→Enforcing an Award or a Judgment in Korea

Winning is a document. Turning it into money in Korea takes one more step in a Korean court.

  • An award made in Korea — enforcement requires a court enforcement decision (집행결정) on application (Arbitration Act, Article 37(2)). The applicant files the award or a copy, with a Korean translation if it is in another language (Article 37(3))
  • An award made abroad — for example in Singapore, London or Paris. Korea has been a party to the New York Convention on foreign arbitral awards since 1973, and Article 39(1) of the Arbitration Act applies the Convention to awards it covers. Korea made two declarations: it applies the Convention only to awards made in another contracting State, and only to disputes from commercial relationships
  • An award outside the Convention — treated like a foreign court judgment (Article 39(2), referring to Article 217 of the Civil Procedure Act and Articles 26 and 27 of the Civil Execution Act)
  • A foreign court judgment — needs a Korean enforcement judgment and must meet the conditions of Article 217 of the Civil Procedure Act, which include reciprocity between the two countries

Once enforceable, the award or judgment is executed like a Korean judgment — see enforcing a judgment in Korea.

→Freezing the Korean Party's Assets First

A Korean company that knows a claim is coming can move money long before an award or judgment arrives. A provisional attachment (가압류) of its bank deposits, receivables or real property can be obtained from a Korean court without notice to the debtor. An arbitration clause does not block this: Article 10 of the Arbitration Act allows a party to an arbitration agreement to apply to the court for interim protective measures before or during the arbitration. The requirements, the security the court asks for and the debtor's counter-moves are explained in provisional attachment in Korea.

→Documents to Gather Now

  • The contract set — signed contract or distribution agreement, purchase orders, order confirmations, pro forma and commercial invoices, and any general terms that were exchanged
  • Shipping documents — bill of lading or air waybill, packing list, certificate of origin, letter of credit documents, customs records
  • Proof of the problem — inspection or survey reports, photos with dates, test results, the written notice of non-conformity and the date it was sent
  • Payment history — remittance records, statements of account, any partial payments or written acknowledgments of the debt
  • Messages — emails and chat messages with the Korean side, exported with dates, not just screenshots
  • The Korean party itself — its exact registered name and business registration number, which make the corporate registry and asset searches possible

→What to Do This Week

  1. Read the dispute clause and the governing-law clause in every document, including the back of the invoice
  2. If the goods are defective, send a written notice specifying the defect now — this deadline runs before any other
  3. Count the limitation period from the due date of each unpaid invoice
  4. Decide on a freeze before the demand letter if there is any sign the Korean side is moving assets
  5. Send a Korean-language demand naming the amount, the deadline and the forum you will use
  6. Choose the forum with enforcement in mind — where are the assets, and will the award or judgment be recognised there?

A cross-border dispute rewards the side that acts in the right order. The merits matter, but the notice, the clock and the freeze are usually decided in the first few weeks.
