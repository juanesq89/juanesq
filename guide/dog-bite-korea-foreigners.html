A Dog Bite (개물림) in Korea: Who Pays, and When It Becomes a Criminal Case

By Juan, Attorney at Law 6 min read English · Español · Português

A dog without a leash runs at you in the park. A neighbour’s dog bites your child in the lift. Or it is your own dog, and the person it bit is now asking for money. Korean law treats a dog bite on two tracks at once: compensation under the Civil Act, and possible charges under the Criminal Act and the Animal Protection Act.

Whichever side you are on, the questions are the same: who is responsible, what can be claimed, and whether the police will be involved. The answers depend less on the dog than on what its owner or walker did, or failed to do, before the bite.

→Compensation: The Owner Must Show They Took Proper Care

Article 759(1) of the Civil Act makes the person in possession of an animal (동물의 점유자) liable for the harm it causes to others, unless they show that they took the care appropriate to the kind and nature of the animal. The burden is on the owner: the injured person does not have to prove carelessness, the owner has to prove care. Article 759(2) extends the same liability to anyone keeping the animal in the owner’s place, such as a dog walker, a pet sitter or a friend minding the dog.

  • What can be claimed: medical and treatment costs, income lost while recovering, and compensation for pain and distress (위자료). Keep every receipt and the doctor’s diagnosis certificate (진단서).
  • Deadline: a tort claim lapses 3 years after you learn of the harm and of the person responsible, and in any case 10 years after the incident. See limitation periods in Korea.
  • Insurance: the owner of a dangerous dog (맹견) must carry insurance covering injury the dog causes to people or animals (Animal Protection Act Art. 23(1)). Other owners may have liability cover under a home or personal policy, so ask.

→When a Bite Becomes a Criminal Case

A bite can also be a crime, and which law applies depends on what the owner failed to do. The Animal Protection Act requires owners of dogs subject to registration (등록대상동물) not to let them leave home without the owner (Art. 16(1)) and, when taking them out, to use a leash or other safety measure meeting the Ministry’s standard and to attach an ID tag (Art. 16(2)). Owners of dangerous dogs (맹견: breeds such as the Tosa, the Pit Bull Terrier and the Rottweiler, as set by Ministry ordinance) must also fit a leash and a muzzle, or use a secure carrier, when out with a dog aged 3 months or older (Art. 21(1)).

The question is rarely what the dog did. It is what the person holding the leash did.

→If You Were Bitten

  1. Get treatment first and ask the hospital for a diagnosis certificate. It is the document both the police and the owner’s insurer will ask for.
  2. Identify the owner or walker before they leave: name, phone number, and a photo of the dog. Ask witnesses for their contact details.
  3. Photograph the wound and the place, including whether the dog was on a leash. Ask building management or nearby shops about CCTV quickly, since recordings are overwritten.
  4. Report it. Call 112 if the owner refuses to give details or the injury is serious. A police report also records the facts for the civil claim. How a victim starts a case is set out in filing a criminal complaint.
  5. Claim the money. Most dog-bite claims settle. If not, a claim of 30 million won or less can go through the small claims procedure, and if the owner is prosecuted, you may ask the criminal court for a compensation order (배상명령).

→If It Was Your Dog

  • Stay, help and exchange details. Leaving makes the civil claim harder to settle and the criminal case harder to explain.
  • Settlement matters, but not equally for every charge. A negligent-injury charge under Criminal Act Art. 266 cannot proceed against the victim’s express wish, so a settlement with a statement that the victim does not want punishment (처벌불원) ends it. The Animal Protection Act offence has no such rule in its text: a settlement weighs in your favour but does not by law close that case. See settlement in Korean criminal matters.
  • The police interview is the one that counts. Since 2 October 2026 prosecutors at the public prosecution office (공소청) decide on indictment from the investigators’ file and do not question suspects themselves, so prepare before you give a statement. A small case often ends in a summary order with a fine, which is still a criminal record and can come up when you renew your stay.
  • Check your insurance for liability cover before you pay out of pocket.
