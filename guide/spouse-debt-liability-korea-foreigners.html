Your Spouse’s Debts in Korea: When Marriage Makes You Liable, and When It Does Not

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

A lender calls you about a loan your husband took out. A card company writes to your home about your wife’s balance. Being married in Korea does not, by itself, make you the debtor. Korean law starts from separate property and separate debts — with one exception for the household, and a second one you create yourself when you sign.

This guide is for the spouse who is being asked to pay. If you are the one who borrowed and your marriage is ending, read it alongside property division in a Korean divorce.

→The Starting Point: What Is Yours Stays Yours

Article 830(1) of the Civil Act (민법) makes property that a spouse owned before the marriage, and property acquired during the marriage in that spouse’s own name, that spouse’s separate property (특유재산). The same logic runs to debts: a loan contract binds the person who signed it. A lender cannot sue you on your spouse’s loan simply because your names are on the same family register.

There is one presumption to keep in mind. Under Article 830(2), property that cannot be shown to belong to either spouse is presumed to be owned by both. That matters later, when a creditor comes for the contents of your home.

→The Exception: Debts for Daily Household Affairs

Article 827(1) gives spouses authority to act for each other in daily household affairs (일상의 가사). Article 832 then attaches liability to it:

When one spouse enters into a legal act with a third party concerning daily household affairs, the other spouse is jointly and severally liable for the resulting obligation.

Joint and several (연대) means the creditor can ask either of you for the whole amount. The article has one way out written into it: there is no liability if you had already told that third party that you would not be responsible.

What counts as “daily household affairs” is decided case by case — courts look at what the transaction was for, how much it was, and how the family actually lives. As a rough guide:

Usually treated as householdUsually outside it
Food, utilities and ordinary living expensesLoans for one spouse’s business
Everyday purchases for the homeLarge loans out of proportion to the family’s living costs
Ordinary costs of raising the childrenGuarantees for someone else’s debt, buying or selling real estate

A borrowed sum is not automatically a household debt because the borrower says it went on rent. The amount, the purpose and the evidence of where the money went all decide it.

→What You Signed Is a Different Question

Most of the cases in which a spouse really does owe the money have nothing to do with Article 832. The spouse signed something.

  • Co-borrower or guarantor. If you signed the loan or a guarantee (보증, 연대보증), you are liable on your own signature, married or not. Article 428-2 requires a guarantee to be expressed in writing bearing the guarantor’s name and seal or signature — which is why lenders ask the spouse to sign.
  • Your name used without your consent. If your spouse signed for you, your seal or ID was used, or a loan was taken out in your name, the authority in Article 827 does not stretch that far. The lender may argue apparent authority (표현대리, Civil Act Art. 126) — that it had good reason to believe your spouse could act for you — so contest it early and in writing.
  • Cards in your name. A family card issued on your account is your debt, whoever used it.

→When a Creditor Comes for the Household Goods

If a creditor with a judgment against your spouse enforces it, the bailiff can seize movable property that your spouse owns jointly with you and that your spouse possesses, alone or together with you (Civil Execution Act, 민사집행법, Art. 190). Combined with the presumption in Article 830(2), this means the television and furniture in a shared home can be seized for one spouse’s debt.

The law gives the other spouse three tools:

  • Priority purchase. At the sale you can attend and declare that you will buy the item first (Art. 206).
  • Your share of the proceeds. If you claim a co-ownership share in a seized item, you can demand payment of your share out of the sale price (Art. 221).
  • Objection by a third party. If the item is yours alone, you can bring a third-party objection suit (제3자이의의 소) against the creditor to stop the enforcement (Art. 48). Receipts and transfer records in your name are what win it.

Real estate and bank accounts in your name are a different matter: a judgment against your spouse does not reach them — unless they were moved into your name to put them out of the creditor’s reach, which the creditor can challenge as a fraudulent transfer (사해행위, Civil Act Art. 406).

→Divorce and Death Change the Picture

Divorce. A debt in your spouse’s name stays your spouse’s debt toward the lender after a divorce. Between the two of you, however, debts taken on for the family are part of the property division calculation. See property division.

Death. When your spouse dies, the heirs take over the deceased’s property rights and obligations from the moment of death (Art. 1005). As a spouse you are an heir, so the debts can become yours through inheritance even though they never were during the marriage. The deadline to renounce or to accept with limited liability is short. Read inheritance in Korea before you pay anything or use the deceased’s money.

→What to Do Now

  1. Do not pay on reflex. Ask the creditor in writing for the contract and for whatever bears your signature.
  2. Check what you signed — loan papers, guarantees, card applications, and anything signed with your seal.
  3. If your name was used without consent, say so in writing to the creditor straight away and keep a copy.
  4. Keep proof of what is yours — receipts and transfers for property in a shared home.
  5. Before you sign a guarantee for your spouse, assume you will be the one paying it.
  6. If your spouse has died, get advice before the inheritance deadline passes.
