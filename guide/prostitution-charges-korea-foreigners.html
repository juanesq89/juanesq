Caught in a Prostitution Case (성매매) in Korea: The Fine, Your Record and Your Visa

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

A massage shop that offered more than a massage. A meeting arranged through a chat app that turned out to be a police sting. A raid on a bar while you were inside. Buying or selling sex is a crime in Korea for both sides, and foreigners are often stopped by the police without having known that. Most adult first cases end with a fine or a conditional decision not to prosecute rather than prison — but which of those you get decides what stays on your record and what immigration sees.

This guide covers the person who paid or agreed to pay, the person who sold, and the very different rule when the other person turns out to be a minor.

→It Is a Crime for Both Sides

The Act on the Punishment of Arranging Sexual Services (성매매알선 등 행위의 처벌에 관한 법률, usually called 성매매처벌법) punishes anyone who sells or buys sex with up to 1 year’s imprisonment, a fine of up to 3 million won, detention (구류) or a petty fine (과료) (Art. 21(1)). It does not matter where you come from or whether it is legal there.

→If the Other Person Was a Minor

Age changes the case completely. Buying sex from a minor is not the one-year offence above but a separate crime under the Act on the Protection of Children and Youth against Sex Offenses, with a minimum of one year in prison or a fine of at least 20 million won. It is also a sexual offence against a minor, which brings consequences after conviction that go far beyond the sentence. In chat-app cases, “they told me they were an adult” is raised almost every time. Whether it helps depends on what you could reasonably have known — and it is the first thing to go through with a lawyer, before any statement.

→How Adult Cases Usually End

For an adult first offence, prison is rare. The realistic outcomes are three, and they are not equal:

  • Conditional suspension of prosecution (기소유예) with an education order. The prosecutor decides not to charge on condition that you attend a re-offending prevention course for buyers, commonly called “John school” (존스쿨). There is no conviction. See 기소유예, 선고유예 and 집행유예 compared.
  • A protection case (보호사건). Where protective measures fit better than punishment, the prosecutor sends the case to the court as a protection case, unless there are special circumstances (Art. 12(1)); a criminal court can do the same after hearing the case (Art. 12(2)). The result is a protective measure, not a criminal sentence.
  • A fine by summary order (약식명령). Paying it means a criminal conviction. You have 7 days from receiving it to ask for a formal trial (Criminal Procedure Act Art. 453(1)). See summary orders in Korea.

The difference between a suspended prosecution and a fine is the difference between no conviction and a criminal record — and it is mostly decided on the police file.

→The Police Interview Is the Statement That Counts

Since 2 October 2026 the police investigate and send the file on; the prosecutor at the public prosecution office (공소청) decides on that file whether to charge and no longer questions suspects. What you say to the police is therefore what the decision is based on. Your rights are in questioned by Korean police.

  • Ask for an interpreter and do not sign a record you cannot read.
  • Do not guess. If you do not remember an amount, a time or a message, say so.
  • Expect to be asked for your phone. In app-arranged cases the chats and transfer records are the core evidence. Before you hand it over “voluntarily”, read police searches and phone seizures in Korea.

→Your Visa

A fine or a suspended prosecution does not deport you by itself. But the Immigration Act allows deportation where a ground for refusing entry arises after entry (Art. 46(1)), and the entry-refusal grounds include conduct likely to harm public order or good morals (Art. 11(1)). In practice a prostitution case is weighed when you extend your stay, change status, or apply for permanent residence or naturalisation, and a conviction appears on a Korean criminal record check. If your job depends on a clean record — teaching in particular — the outcome without a conviction is worth fighting for. See deportation and entry bans and departure bans.

→If You Were the One Selling

If someone took your passport, kept you in debt, threatened you or brought you to Korea for this, the law treats you as a victim of prostitution (성매매피해자) and does not punish you (Art. 6(1)). Say so at the very start, ask for an interpreter, and ask to be put in touch with a support organisation. See also an employer holding your passport.

→What to Do Now

  1. Do not delete chats or apps. It does not remove what the platform or the other phone holds, and it looks worse.
  2. Find out exactly what you are accused of — adult or minor, buying or arranging — before the interview.
  3. Get advice before the first statement, aiming for an outcome without a conviction where the facts allow it.
  4. Check your visa calendar. An extension that falls due while the case is open needs planning.
  5. If a summary order arrives, count 7 days from the day you received it.
