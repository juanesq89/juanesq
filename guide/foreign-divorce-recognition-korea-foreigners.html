Divorced Abroad, Still Married in Korea: Getting a Foreign Divorce Recognized (외국 이혼판결의 승인)

By Juan, Attorney at Law 8 min read English · Español · Português

A divorce decree from a court in Madrid, São Paulo or California ends the marriage where it was granted. In Korea, the marriage stays on the record until someone files that decree — and until then, the law treats the couple as married.

The call usually comes years after the divorce. A Korean client wants to remarry and the registry office refuses, because their family relations register still shows a spouse. A foreign client learns that their former Korean spouse has died and that they appear, on paper, as a surviving spouse and heir. A Korean parent tries to update a child's records and discovers that nothing about the foreign custody order has reached Korea.

None of these people did anything wrong. They obtained a valid divorce abroad and assumed it would travel by itself. It does not. Korea keeps marital status in the family relations register (가족관계등록부), and that register changes only when a report is filed.

→When This Matters

The Korean register exists only for Korean nationals. The question therefore arises whenever at least one spouse is, or was, a Korean citizen:

  • A Korean and a foreign national who married and divorced abroad
  • A Korean couple living overseas who divorced in a foreign court
  • A foreign national who married a Korean in Korea, later moved abroad and divorced there

Until the divorce is recorded, the consequences are real. The Korean spouse cannot register a new marriage. The former spouse may be treated as a spouse for inheritance purposes. Records about children, custody and parental authority stay out of date, which surfaces later — at a passport application, a school enrollment, or a property transaction.

→The Four Conditions for Recognition

Korea does not re-try a foreign divorce. A final foreign judgment is recognized automatically, without a separate Korean judgment, if it meets the conditions in the Civil Procedure Act (민사소송법, Article 217), which also apply to family judgments:

  1. Jurisdiction. The foreign court must have had international jurisdiction under the principles Korean law accepts — typically, a real connection between the case and that country, such as the couple's residence there
  2. Proper service on the defendant. The spouse who lost must have been served in time to defend, by a method other than service by publication (공시송달) — or must have actually taken part in the case
  3. Public policy. The judgment and the procedure must not offend Korean public policy
  4. Reciprocity. Judgments of the Korean courts must be recognized in that country on comparable conditions

In most divorces between people who actually lived in the country where they divorced and who both took part in the proceedings, these conditions are met without difficulty. The second condition is the one that causes trouble: a divorce granted in default, after the Korean spouse was served by publication or never learned of the case, may not be recognized.

The test is not whether the divorce was fair. It is whether the other spouse was given a real chance to be heard.

→Filing the Divorce in Korea

Because recognition is automatic, the practical step is administrative: a divorce report (이혼신고) is filed with a district or city office in Korea, or through a Korean embassy or consulate abroad. The registry official checks the documents against the conditions above.

The documents typically required are:

  • A certified copy of the foreign judgment
  • Proof that it is final (a certificate of finality, or the equivalent under that country's procedure)
  • Proof of how the Korean defendant was served, or that they took part in the case — unless the Korean spouse was the one who filed
  • A Korean translation of each document

A divorce that becomes final abroad should be reported without delay. A late report may lead to a small administrative fine, but it remains possible — and better late than never, because the consequences of an unrecorded divorce do not lapse with time.

If the registry office declines to record the divorce — most often because service on the defendant is unclear — there are two routes. One is to challenge the refusal through the court. The other, where the foreign judgment genuinely may not qualify, is to obtain a divorce in a Korean court, where the foreign proceedings can be part of the evidence.

→Divorce by Agreement Abroad

Some countries allow a divorce without a court — by registration with a civil registry or a notary. Whether Korea accepts such a divorce depends on the law that governs the divorce, which in turn depends on the spouses' nationality and where they lived. A Korean couple, for instance, cannot simply bypass the Korean procedure for divorce by agreement, which requires confirmation of intent before a Korean family court; Korean nationals abroad do this through a Korean consulate.

If your divorce abroad was not granted by a court, check before you rely on it whether it can be recorded in Korea. Discovering the problem when a remarriage is refused is the expensive way to learn it.

→What Recording Does Not Do

Recording the divorce changes marital status — and, where the judgment decides it, the record of who holds parental authority over the children. It does not make the money parts of the judgment enforceable.

Alimony, child support and property division ordered abroad can be enforced against assets in Korea only after a Korean court grants an enforcement judgment (집행판결) under the Civil Execution Act. The court checks the same four conditions; it does not reopen the merits. Where the former spouse holds property, salary or a lease deposit in Korea, that application is how an unpaid foreign maintenance order becomes collectable here.

→Practical Points

  • Do not assume your former spouse has filed. Check the register — a Korean national can obtain their own certificates easily, and so can a lawyer acting for them
  • Keep the full foreign file, including the proof of service, not only the final decree; the decree alone is often not enough
  • If you are a foreign national whose stay in Korea depended on the marriage, the divorce affects your visa from the moment it is final, not from the date it is recorded
  • If children are involved, record the custody arrangements at the same time, so that the child's documents match reality
