Renting a Shop or Office in Korea: The Commercial Lease Act (상가임대차보호법) for Foreign Business Owners

By Juan, Attorney at Law 9 min read English · Español · Português

The lease is usually the largest commitment a foreign business owner makes in Korea, and it is signed in the first weeks — before the owner knows which of its terms the law will override and which it will enforce to the letter.

I see the same two moments again and again. The first is the signing: a restaurant, a language school, a small trading office, a contract in Korean, a deposit that represents most of the owner's savings, and a landlord's agent explaining that this is the standard form. The second comes two or five years later, when the landlord announces that the rent is going up by a third, or that the lease will not be renewed, or that the owner may not hand the shop to a buyer who has offered to pay for the business.

Korea has a statute written for exactly those moments: the Commercial Building Lease Protection Act (상가건물 임대차보호법). It applies to foreign tenants in the same way as to Korean ones. What it does not do is apply itself — most of its protections depend on steps the tenant must take, and on deadlines the tenant must notice.

→Who the Act Protects

The Act covers leases of buildings used for a business that is registered with the tax office — a shop, a café, an office, a clinic, a school. Two points decide how much of it applies to you.

  • Business registration (사업자등록). Protection against a new owner of the building begins from the day after you both take possession of the premises and apply for business registration. No registration, no protection against a buyer of the building — and a foreign national can register a business only if their status of stay allows business activity
  • The size of the lease. The Act converts your lease into a single figure, the "converted deposit" (환산보증금): the deposit plus the monthly rent multiplied by 100. Below the regional threshold set by decree — in Seoul currently 900 million won — the whole Act applies. Above it, a core of protections still applies, but several others, including the cap on rent increases, do not
ProtectionApplies above the threshold?
Protection against a new owner (대항력)Yes

Termination only for three months of arrearsYes
5% cap on rent increasesNo — increases are judged by what is reasonable
Priority repayment of the deposit at auctionNo

The practical message is to do the arithmetic before signing. The converted-deposit figure decides which column you are in, and it is calculated the same way whatever the contract calls the payments.

→The Right to Renew — Ten Years in Total

The heart of the Act is the tenant's right to demand renewal (계약갱신요구권). You exercise it by telling the landlord, between six months and one month before the lease ends, that you want to renew. The landlord may refuse only for a reason listed in the Act, and you may keep renewing until the total period, counted from the first lease, reaches ten years.

The listed reasons for refusal are narrower than landlords often suggest:

  1. Arrears that have reached three months' rent at some point during the lease (it need not be three consecutive months)
  2. A lease obtained by deception, or a sublease made without the landlord's consent
  3. Serious damage to the premises caused intentionally or by gross negligence
  4. Reconstruction or major redevelopment of the building that meets the conditions in the Act — for example, a plan disclosed to the tenant when the lease was signed, or a safety risk
  5. Other serious breaches of the tenant's obligations

What is not on the list: that the landlord has found a tenant who will pay more, that a relative wants the space, or that the building has been sold. A new owner steps into the old owner's position, renewal right included, where you have possession and business registration.

Telling the landlord in time is the whole of the right. A renewal demand made two weeks before the end of the lease is a request, not a right.

Send the demand in a form you can prove — a content-certified letter (내용증명) is the conventional method — and keep the delivery record.

→Rent Increases

Within the threshold, a rent or deposit increase on renewal, or during the lease, may not exceed 5% of the current amount, and may not be repeated within a year of the last one. A landlord who demands 20% on renewal is not entitled to it; the renewal takes place on the same terms with, at most, the permitted increase.

Above the threshold there is no fixed cap, but the landlord still cannot impose an arbitrary figure as a condition of renewal: the increase must be reasonable in light of taxes, costs and comparable rents, and a disputed figure can be decided by a court.

If neither side says anything before the lease ends, it renews automatically on the same terms for one year (within the threshold). During an automatically renewed term, the tenant may give notice at any time, and the lease ends three months after the landlord receives it.

→Key Money: The Value of a Running Business

Key money (권리금) is the price a new tenant pays the outgoing tenant for the business itself — the location's goodwill, customers, fit-out and equipment. It is not paid to the landlord and does not appear in the lease, but it is often worth more than the deposit.

The Act protects your chance to recover it. From six months before the lease ends until it ends, the landlord may not obstruct your efforts to hand over to a new tenant who will pay you key money. Obstruction includes demanding key money from the new tenant, demanding rent far above the market level, or refusing without good reason to sign with the tenant you have found.

The landlord may refuse a proposed tenant for legitimate reasons — a tenant without the means to pay the rent, for instance — and the protection does not apply to large retail complexes. Where the landlord obstructs without a valid reason, you may claim damages, capped at the lower of the key money the new tenant agreed to pay and the key money the business was worth when the lease ended. The claim must be brought within three years of the end of the lease.

What makes or breaks this claim is evidence that you actually found a tenant: a signed key-money agreement, or at least written proof of the offer, and of the landlord's response. Owners who simply tell the landlord they are leaving and hope for a buyer later have nothing to claim.

→The End of the Lease

When the lease ends, two obligations meet: the landlord must return the deposit, and you must hand back the premises in their original condition (원상회복). Disputes about how far "original condition" reaches — the floor you laid, the kitchen you installed, the signage — are the most common reason deposits are withheld. The lease and the condition report at the start decide most of them, which is why both deserve attention on day one.

If the deposit is not returned and you need to move out, you can apply for a lease registration order (임차권등기명령), which preserves your protection after you leave. A landlord who simply does not pay can be sued, and where there is a risk that the building is being sold or mortgaged further, a provisional attachment may come first.

→Before You Sign

For a foreign business owner the checklist is short, and almost all of it is done before the contract:

  • Confirm that your visa permits the business you plan to run, and that the premises can be registered for it
  • Read the building register and the property register (등기부등본) for mortgages that rank ahead of your deposit
  • Calculate the converted deposit and know which protections you have
  • Make sure special clauses (특약) do not waive rights the Act gives you — clauses that are less favorable to the tenant than the Act are generally void, but it is better not to argue the point later
  • Diarize the six-month window before the end of the lease
