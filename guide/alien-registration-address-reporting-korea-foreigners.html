Alien Registration (외국인등록) and Address Reporting in Korea: The Small Duties That Cost People Their Status

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English

Nobody loses their visa because they moved apartments. They lose it because they moved apartments, said nothing for two years, and then applied for something that required a clean record.

Korean immigration law does not only ask you to hold a valid status. It asks you to keep the government's picture of you accurate — where you live, who you work for, what your passport number is. These reporting duties (신고의무, singo uimu) sit in the Immigration Act (출입국관리법, chulipguk gwallibeop), carry deadlines measured in days, and are enforced by fines (과태료, gwataeryo) rather than by anyone knocking on your door. That is exactly why they are missed.

→Alien Registration (외국인등록): The First Deadline

A foreign national staying in Korea for more than 90 days must complete alien registration (외국인등록, oegugin deungnok) at the immigration office with jurisdiction over their address. The clock runs from entry, and the resulting alien registration card (외국인등록증, oegugin deungnokjeung) is what actually makes ordinary life possible.

Without it there is no bank account worth having, no mobile phone contract, no health insurance enrolment, and no way to prove who you are at a counter that will not accept a passport alone. Practically, the card is your identity in Korea; the resident registration number equivalent printed on it is what every Korean system is built around.

  • Where — the immigration office (출입국·외국인청, chulipguk oegugincheong) covering your residence, generally by appointment booked through the HiKorea system
  • What to bring — passport, application form, a photograph, the fee, and documentation of your address such as a lease (임대차계약서, imdaecha gyeyakseo) or a dormitory confirmation
  • Who is exempt — short-term visitors and certain statuses, but the exemption is narrower than most people assume, and holding a long-term visa in your passport does not remove the duty
  • Carrying it — the card is meant to be carried, and a request to show it during a police or immigration check is lawful

→The 14-Day Rule After You Move

When a registered foreign national moves, the change of residence must be reported (체류지 변경신고, cheryuji byeongyeong singo) within 14 days. The report can be made at the immigration office or, more conveniently, at the community service centre (주민센터, jumin senteo) for the new address — the same counter where Koreans file their move-in report.

Fourteen days is not a grace period before something bad happens. It is the period after which something has already happened, quietly, on your file.

This is the single most commonly breached duty I see, and the reason is structural. Nothing goes wrong on day fifteen. The landlord does not report you, the employer does not check, and the mail that matters is electronic. The consequence arrives later, in a form that is difficult to argue with: a fine assessed at the counter when you finally do report, and an entry on a record that an officer will read while deciding something discretionary about your future.

→The Other Reports People Forget

Address is the well-known one. It is not the only one, and the others carry the same fourteen-day rhythm.

ChangeWhat Has To Be Done
Passport renewed or replacedReport the new passport number and expiry — the immigration record still points at a document that no longer exists
Name, nationality, date of birth correctedReport the change with authenticated supporting documents
Change of workplace (근무처 변경, geunmucheo byeongyeong)Depending on the status, either advance permission or a report — treating a permission requirement as a mere report is a serious error
Losing the alien registration cardApply for reissuance; continuing without it is its own problem at every check
Leaving Korea permanentlyReturn the card at departure where required, and settle the status properly rather than simply flying out

The workplace item deserves emphasis. For several employment statuses, moving to a new employer without the required permission is not a paperwork slip — it is working outside the scope of your status, which is a different and much heavier category of violation than a late address report.

→What a Missed Report Actually Costs

The immediate cost is a fine (과태료), scaled by how late the report is and whether there is a history of the same lapse. For a first, short delay the amount is usually modest, and people conclude from that number that the matter is trivial.

The real cost is not the fine. It is what the record does afterwards. Korean immigration decisions on extension, on a change of status, on permanent residency (영주권, yeongjugwon), and on naturalisation (귀화, gwihwa) all involve discretion, and repeated reporting violations are read as an indication that the applicant does not comply with the conditions of their stay. An applicant with an otherwise strong file can be refused on a record built entirely out of small, forgotten forms.

  • A single late report, promptly cured — normally a fine and little more
  • A pattern of late or missing reports — a visible compliance history that surfaces at every discretionary decision
  • False information in a report — treated far more seriously than lateness, and capable of putting the status itself at risk
  • Unreported change of workplace where permission was required — potentially a status violation, with consequences up to departure orders

→If You Are Already Late

Late is a fixable condition. Unreported is not, because it keeps compounding while you decide what to do.

  1. File the report now, at the community service centre (주민센터) for your current address or at the immigration office, and expect the fine to be assessed at that moment rather than later
  2. Bring the lease and the move-in evidence, because the date you actually moved determines the amount and you want it documented rather than estimated
  3. Check the other duties at the same time — passport number, employer, card validity — since people who missed one usually missed two
  4. Take advice before a renewal or a permanent residency application if the history is long, so that the file explains the record instead of leaving an officer to interpret it

Where a delay had a genuine reason — hospitalisation, a landlord who refused to provide documents, a period spent abroad — that reason is worth putting in writing rather than leaving unsaid. It does not erase the violation, but a record that carries an explanation reads very differently from one that carries only dates.
