The F-2-7 Points-Based Residence Visa (거주) in Korea: How You Are Scored, and What Changes When You Have It

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English

On a work visa, your right to stay in Korea belongs to your employer. On an F-2, it belongs to you. The distance between the two is a points table — and most people who could cross it never check their score.

Korea's residence status (거주, geoju) in the F-2 category includes a points-based route, commonly referred to by its sub-code F-2-7. It is aimed at foreign residents already living and working in Korea who accumulate enough points across education, income, Korean ability, and other criteria. It is not a new-arrival visa, and it is not tied to any one employer.

→What the F-2 Residence Status (거주) Actually Buys You

The practical differences from an employment status such as E-7 (specific activity, 특정활동) or E-2 (foreign language instruction, 회화지도) are structural rather than cosmetic.

On an Employer-Tied Work VisaOn F-2 Residence Status (거주)
Your status depends on one sponsoring employerYour status is personal — it survives leaving a job
Changing workplace needs permission or reporting, and can be refusedYou can generally work without employer sponsorship, subject to the conditions on your permit
Losing the job puts your status at immediate riskLosing the job is a financial problem, not an immigration emergency
The road to permanent residency (영주, yeongju) is longThe points route is treated as a shortened path toward permanent residency

A work visa asks who employs you. A residence status asks who you are. That is the entire difference, and it is worth more than most salary negotiations.

→How the Scoring Works

Eligibility is assessed against a points table (점수제, jeomsuje) published by the immigration authorities. The structure has been consistent for years: a set of core scored items, a set of additional or bonus items, and a pass mark applied to the total. The pass mark has generally been 80 points out of a 120-point core table, with bonus items counted on top.

  • Age — scored on bands, with applicants in their late twenties and thirties typically scoring highest
  • Education — degree level, with additional weight where the degree was obtained in Korea
  • Korean language ability — through the TOPIK examination (한국어능력시험, hangugeo neungnyeok siheom) or completion of the Social Integration Program (사회통합프로그램, sahoe tonghap peurogeuraem), which is often the single most efficient way to add points
  • Annual income — assessed on documented Korean income, which is why the income certificate you file matters as much as what you were actually paid
  • Additional items — such as work experience in Korea, volunteer activity, awards, and other categories that the notice specifies

The exact items, weights, and thresholds are set by ministry notice and are revised from time to time. Treat any table you find online — including a summary like this one — as an orientation, and confirm the current version before you build an application around it.

→Who Can Apply From Where

This is where most people are quietly disqualified before scoring even begins. The route is open to holders of certain statuses already in Korea, not to everyone with a good CV.

  • Professional employment and study-derived statuses — the E-series professional categories and certain D-series statuses are the usual starting points
  • A period of lawful stay in that status is generally required before you can apply, so the clock starts from when you entered the qualifying status, not from when you first arrived
  • A clean immigration and criminal record — a conviction, including a fine imposed by summary order, or a history of reporting failures can end an application that scores well on paper
  • Certain statuses are excluded outright, and being out of status at the moment of application is fatal regardless of score

→Where Applications Actually Fail

In practice the refusals I see cluster around evidence, not eligibility. The applicant qualified; the file did not prove it.

  • Income proved by a payslip instead of a tax record — what carries weight is documented income confirmed by the tax office, typically a certificate of income amount (소득금액증명, sodeuk geumaek jeungmyeong) or withholding receipt. Cash top-ups and unreported income score nothing
  • Language points claimed from an expired certificate — TOPIK results have a validity period, and a certificate that lapsed a month before filing is worth zero
  • Degrees without apostille (아포스티유, apostiyu) or consular confirmation (영사확인, yeongsa hwagin) — a foreign diploma that is not authenticated is not evidence
  • Reporting gaps — an unreported change of address or workplace surfaces in your record at exactly the moment you are asking for a discretionary upgrade
  • Filing too close to expiry — processing takes time, and an application submitted days before your current status ends leaves no room to respond to a request for supplementary documents

→After F-2-7: The Route to Permanent Residency (영주)

The points route exists partly as a feeder into permanent residency (영주, yeongju), the F-5 status. Holders of F-2-7 are generally able to apply for permanent residency after a shorter period of continuous residence than the ordinary route requires — commonly cited as three years in the status, subject to income, conduct, and residence conditions being met throughout.

That is why the F-2-7 application is worth treating as the first step of a two-step plan rather than an end in itself. Decisions made now — whether to complete the Social Integration Program (사회통합프로그램), how income is documented, whether to keep continuous residence unbroken — are the same decisions the permanent residency file will be judged on later.

→How to Approach It

  1. Score yourself honestly against the current notice, not against a blog post from three years ago, and identify the two or three items where you are closest to a higher band
  2. Fix the cheapest gaps first — language points through TOPIK or the Social Integration Program (사회통합프로그램) are usually the fastest points available to someone already living here
  3. Clean up your record before applying — bring address and workplace reporting up to date rather than hoping it goes unnoticed
  4. Build the income evidence deliberately, using tax-confirmed documents, and be aware that a year of underreported income can cost you the application
  5. File with months of status remaining, so that a request for additional documents is an inconvenience rather than a crisis

The people who move onto F-2-7 are rarely the highest earners. They are the ones who looked at the table two years early, saw that they were fifteen points short, and spent those two years closing the gap on purpose.
