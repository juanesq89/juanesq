Your Baby Was Born in Korea and Is Not Korean: Birth Registration (출생신고), Status, and the 90-Day Clock

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English

The hospital gives you a birth certificate and sends you home. Nothing else happens automatically. Your child now exists in Korea with no nationality recorded here, no status of stay, and a deadline nobody mentioned.

Korea determines nationality by parentage rather than by place of birth. A child born on Korean soil to two foreign parents does not become Korean, and cannot be entered in the Korean family relations register (가족관계등록부, gajokgwangye deungnokbu) — that register exists for Korean nationals. What the birth creates instead is a set of obligations running to two different governments at once, on two different clocks.

→The Two Registrations Are Not the Same Thing

People arrive at my office having done one of these and assuming it covered the other. It does not.

What It IsWhere It Happens
Recording the birth for your own country — the basis for the child's passport and nationalityYour embassy or consulate in Korea, under your country's rules
Obtaining the child's right to remain in Korea — grant of status of stay (체류자격 부여, cheryujagyeok buyeo)Korean immigration, under the Immigration Act

The hospital's birth certificate (출생증명서, chulsaeng jeungmyeongseo) is the document that starts both. Get several original copies before you leave, and have it translated early — you will be asked for it repeatedly, and hospitals become slower to reissue as time passes.

→The 90-Day Clock on the Korean Side

A foreign national born in Korea who intends to stay must apply for a grant of status of stay (체류자격 부여, cheryujagyeok buyeo) within 90 days of birth. This is the deadline that catches new parents, because nothing in the hospital discharge process mentions it.

  • The status granted usually tracks the parents' status. A newborn is generally brought under a dependent or family-related category consistent with the parents' own residence position
  • Missing the deadline is treated as a status violation — fines are common, and the irregularity attaches to the child's record from the beginning of their life here
  • Alien registration (외국인등록, oegugin deungnok) follows for a child staying beyond the short-term period, done by the parent as legal representative
  • Leaving Korea does not solve it. Departing with a child who has no recorded status creates its own problem at the airport, and re-entry is where it surfaces

A newborn cannot overstay through any fault of their own. The record does not care — it simply shows an irregular status that the child will carry into every future application.

→When One Parent Is Korean

Here the analysis changes completely. Korean nationality passes through either parent, so a child with one Korean parent is generally Korean from birth — and the correct step is a Korean birth report (출생신고, chulsaengsingo) entering the child in the family relations register, not an immigration application.

The complication is when the parents are not married, and the Korean parent is the father. Nationality through the father in that situation generally depends on legal acknowledgment of paternity (인지, inji):

  • Acknowledgment before birth generally produces Korean nationality from birth, with the child entered in the register directly
  • Acknowledgment after birth follows a different route, under which nationality is acquired by a separate reporting step rather than automatically
  • Where the father refuses to acknowledge the child, a paternity action (인지청구, injicheonggu) exists — and it is the same proceeding that establishes child support obligations
  • Where the mother is married to someone else, the presumption of paternity may attach to the husband, and that presumption has to be addressed before the register can reflect reality

These cases are worth handling early. Every month of delay adds a child with no passport, no insurance, and no lawful basis to remain.

→The Practical Costs of Delay

Parents who postpone usually do so because one document is missing, or because they are afraid of what contact with the authorities will trigger. The consequences compound quietly:

  • Health insurance. A newborn is normally covered as a dependent of an enrolled parent, but coverage depends on the child being properly recorded — until then, medical costs are out of pocket
  • Childcare, vaccination, and school. Access is administered against identifying numbers the child does not yet have
  • Travel. No embassy registration means no passport; no Korean status means no lawful exit and return
  • Statelessness risk. Some countries restrict nationality transmission by descent, or require registration within a set period. If that window closes while you are also outside the Korean system, the child can end up recognized by neither state

That last risk is the one worth acting on immediately, because it is the only one that becomes genuinely difficult to reverse. If your own country limits nationality by descent, find that rule out in the first weeks — not after the deadline you did not know existed.

→If the Parents' Own Status Is the Problem

A significant share of these cases involve parents who are themselves out of status, and who avoid every office for that reason. It is an understandable calculation and usually the wrong one.

The child's position is separate from the parents' violation, and the consequences of leaving a child unregistered are permanent in a way that a parent's own fine or departure order is not. There are ways to approach this that address the child's registration and the parents' exposure as one plan rather than as a gamble — but that requires knowing, before you walk into an office, what the realistic range of outcomes is. Going in blind, or not going in at all, are both worse than getting that assessment first.
