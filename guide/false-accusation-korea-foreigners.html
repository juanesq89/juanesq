Falsely Accused in Korea: False Accusation (무고죄) and How to Clear Your Name

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

A former partner tells the police something that never happened. A business contact files a fraud complaint to pressure you into paying. You get a call from a police station about a case you have never heard of. In Korea, knowingly reporting false facts to get someone punished is a crime of its own: false accusation (무고, mugo).

There are two sides to this guide. If you are the one accused, the first job is your own case, not the counter-attack. If your case has already been closed, the question becomes whether the complaint against you was a false accusation in the legal sense, and what you can do about it. The two must be handled in that order.

→What False Accusation (무고) Means Under Korean Law

Article 156 of the Criminal Act punishes a person who, with the purpose of having another person receive criminal punishment or disciplinary action, reports false facts to a public office or a public official. The penalty is up to 10 years' imprisonment or a fine of up to 15 million won. Three parts of that wording do most of the work:

  • False facts. The report has to state things that did not happen. A complaint that was true but could not be proven is not described by this article.
  • The purpose. The report must be made to get someone punished, criminally or through disciplinary action.
  • To the authorities. The article speaks of a public office or public official, such as the police. A story spread among friends or online is not 무고; it may be defamation (명예훼손) instead.

→If You Are the One Accused: Your Case Comes First

Being certain the accusation is false does not make the investigation go away. The police will treat the complaint like any other until the evidence says otherwise. Since 2 October 2026 the prosecutor at the public prosecution office (공소청) decides whether to indict on the basis of the police file and does not question suspects, so the police interview is the statement that counts.

  1. Do not contact the accuser to "sort it out". Messages asking them to withdraw, however polite, end up in the file and can be read as pressure.
  2. Secure your own evidence now: messages and call logs, transport and card records, photos with timestamps, and the names of people who were with you. Footage from security cameras is not kept forever, so ask early.
  3. Prepare before the interview. Know what you are accused of and under which article, and give one consistent account. Your rights are set out in questioned by Korean police.
  4. Do not file a false accusation complaint as a reflex. If your own case later turns against you, a counter-complaint built on "it never happened" can be examined as a false report itself.
  5. Think about travel and your visa. An open criminal case can lead to a departure ban (출국정지), and a conviction can affect visa renewals.

A complaint that could not be proven is not the same as a false accusation. 무고 needs a lie, told to the authorities, to get you punished.

→How Your Case Ends, and How Long It Can Stay Open

If the police conclude there is no crime, they do not send the case to the prosecutor (불송치, non-referral). That is good news, but it is not final on the day you hear it:

  • The accuser is told and can object. The police must notify the complainant in writing within 7 days of the non-referral (Criminal Procedure Act Art. 245-6). The complainant may object to the head of the police station within 3 months of that notice, and the case then goes to the prosecutor (Art. 245-7).
  • The prosecutor can send it back. If the prosecutor finds the non-referral unlawful or unjustified, they can demand a reinvestigation, as a rule within 3 months of receiving the file (Art. 245-8).
  • If the case does reach the prosecutor and ends without an indictment (불기소), the complainant can still appeal (항고) under Article 57 of the Public Prosecution Office Act.

Keep every notice you receive. The written reasons for closing your case are the most useful document you will have if you decide to act against the accuser.

→Filing a False Accusation Complaint Against the Accuser

The person who was falsely accused is the victim of the 무고 and can file a criminal complaint (고소) like any other victim (Criminal Procedure Act Art. 223). A few things decide whether that complaint goes anywhere:

  • Proof that the facts were false, and that the accuser knew it. The closing of your case helps, but it is not enough by itself: a case can close because the evidence was thin, not because the story was invented. What you need is evidence that the reported events did not happen, such as records placing you somewhere else or messages contradicting the account.
  • There is no hurry measured in months. 무고 carries a maximum of 10 years, so the limitation period for prosecution is 10 years (Criminal Procedure Act Art. 249(1)(iii)). Waiting until your own case is closed costs nothing.
  • A confession changes the penalty. If the accuser confesses or turns themselves in before the judgment or disciplinary decision in the case they reported becomes final, the court must reduce or remit the punishment (Criminal Act Arts. 157 and 153). This is one reason accusers sometimes withdraw their story once they face a complaint of their own.

Money is a separate track. Legal fees, lost income and the harm of being accused are claimed in a civil action for damages (Civil Act Art. 750), which can run alongside or after the criminal complaint.

→If You Filed a Complaint That Did Not Succeed

The same rule protects honest complainants. Reporting what you believe happened, and failing to prove it, is not 무고. The article requires false facts reported with the purpose of getting someone punished. Where it goes wrong is a complaint used as leverage in a money dispute, with facts stretched to make it look criminal. If you are thinking of filing, read filing a criminal complaint in Korea first.
