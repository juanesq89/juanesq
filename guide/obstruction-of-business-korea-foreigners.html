A Scene in a Bar or Shop in Korea: Minor Offence or Obstruction of Business (업무방해)?

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

A dispute over the bill, one drink too many, a manager who will not listen. The same night can be booked in two very different ways in Korea: as a minor offence closed with a ticket, or as obstruction of business — a crime that carries up to five years.

The sequence is familiar. The police are called to a bar, a convenience store or a restaurant, everyone is taken to the station, and a few days later a summons arrives naming an offence you may never have heard of: obstruction of business (업무방해, eommu banghae).

The name sounds administrative. It is not. It sits in the Criminal Act right after the offence of damaging someone's credit (신용훼손), with the same penalty.

→Two Laws for the Same Night

Minor offence (경범죄)Obstruction of business (업무방해)
LawMinor Offenses Punishment Act, Article 3Criminal Act, Article 314

Usual routeA fine notice (범칙금) from the policePolice investigation, then a prosecutor's decision

The Minor Offenses Act also has its own, lighter "obstruction of business" for disrupting someone's work through mischief or pranks — a fine of up to 200,000 won, detention or a petty fine (Article 3(2), item 3). The serious offence is the one in the Criminal Act.

→When Noise Becomes "Force"

"Force" in Article 314 is not limited to violence. Korean courts read it as any pressure capable of overwhelming the free will of the people running the business. Shouting at staff for a long time, threatening them, throwing or breaking things, blocking the counter, or refusing to leave while customers walk out can all be argued as force.

What decides the charge in practice is less the words used than the effect: how long it lasted, whether the shop or bar actually stopped working, whether customers left, and whether staff were frightened. A short, loud complaint that ends when the manager answers is a different case from half an hour that closed the place for the evening.

Other offences often travel with it:

  • Breaking a glass, a door or a screen — property damage (재물손괴, Criminal Act Article 366): up to 3 years or a fine of up to 7 million won
  • Pushing or hitting someone — assault (폭행, Article 260): up to 2 years, a fine of up to 5 million won, detention or a petty fine
  • Leaving without paying for food or a taxi ride without a good reason — a minor offence (Minor Offenses Act Article 3(1), item 39)

→At the Police Station, Calm Is a Legal Matter

A case can get worse at the station. Being drunk and rowdy in a government office, including a police station, is a separate minor offence with a higher ceiling: a fine of up to 600,000 won, detention or a petty fine (Minor Offenses Act Article 3(3), item 1).

Pushing, hitting or threatening a police officer who is on duty is obstruction of the performance of official duties (공무집행방해, Criminal Act Article 136): up to 5 years' imprisonment or a fine of up to 10 million won. At that point the original argument about the bill is no longer the main case.

→The Fine Notice — and When You Do Not Get One

For a plain minor offence, the police can issue a written fine notice (통고처분). You pay within 10 days; if you miss that, you have 20 more days with a 20% surcharge; after that the case goes to a summary trial (즉결심판) before a judge, unless you pay with a 50% surcharge before it is filed. Once the fine is paid, you cannot be punished again for the same act (Minor Offenses Act Articles 8 and 9).

The notice is not available to everyone. The Act excludes people whose conduct had a victim, habitual offenders, and people whose address or identity is not certain — which can include a short-term visitor (Articles 6 and 7). In those cases the matter goes to a judge instead.

A summary trial is a court proceeding, not a ticket. It is also different from a summary order (약식명령), a written procedure that can impose a criminal fine for an offence such as obstruction of business without a trial.

→Settlement Helps, But It Does Not End the Case

Simple assault cannot be prosecuted against the victim's expressed wishes (Article 260(3)), so a settlement can close that part. Obstruction of business has no such rule. The owner's forgiveness does not end the case by law.

It still matters a great deal. Paying for the damage and the lost business, and a written statement from the owner that they do not want you punished, are among the strongest factors when a prosecutor decides whether to charge and how. Approach the owner through the police or a lawyer — not by going back to the bar the next night to apologise in person.

→What to Do in the First Days

  1. Ask for an interpreter before you give a statement, and do not sign a statement you have not had read back to you in a language you understand
  2. Ask the police to secure the CCTV from the premises, especially if the staff were also aggressive or the dispute started over a real problem with the bill
  3. Keep your receipt, card statement and messages from that night, and the names of anyone who was with you
  4. Get advice before the second interview, where the charge — minor offence or Article 314 — is usually settled

→Your Visa

A paid minor-offence notice and a criminal conviction for obstruction of business are very different records. A criminal fine or sentence can be weighed when you extend your stay, change status, or apply for permanent residence or naturalization. Much of the defence work is steering the case toward the outcome that leaves the visa intact.
