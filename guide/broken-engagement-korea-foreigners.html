Broken Engagement in Korea (약혼 해제): Who Keeps the Gifts and Who Pays Damages

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

The wedding hall is booked, the families have met, the rings and gifts have changed hands — and then one of you calls it off. Korean law treats an engagement as a real legal relationship. It cannot make anyone marry, but it does decide who owes money for the wreckage and who keeps what was given.

This guide is for couples where at least one partner is a foreigner and the engagement ended before the marriage was registered. If you had already lived together as a married couple without registering, read common-law marriage (사실혼) as well — the rules on ending that relationship are close, but not the same.

→An Engagement Is a Legal Promise — With One Limit

An adult may freely enter into an engagement (약혼) under Article 800 of the Civil Act (민법). At 18 a person can become engaged with the consent of their parents or guardian (Art. 801). The Civil Act sets no form for it: no document, ceremony or registration is required, so whether an engagement existed is a question of evidence — the proposal, meeting the families, rings, messages, wedding bookings.

The limit is in Article 803:

An engagement cannot be enforced by a claim for specific performance.

No court in Korea will order anyone to go through with a wedding. Everything that follows is about money and property, not about saving the marriage.

→The Grounds for Ending It (Art. 804)

Either side can walk away in practice, but whether ending the engagement was justified decides who pays. Article 804 lets a party rescind the engagement if the other party:

GroundWhat the article says
1was sentenced, after the engagement, to suspension of qualifications or a heavier penalty
2was placed under adult or limited guardianship after the engagement
3has a venereal disease, an incurable mental illness or another incurable disease
4became engaged to, or married, someone else after the engagement
5had sexual relations with someone else after the engagement
6has been missing, alive or dead unknown, for a year or more since the engagement
7refuses to marry, or keeps putting the wedding off, without good reason
8gives any other serious reason

Ground 8 is where most real disputes land — lies about marital history, debts, job or nationality status, violence, or a family that makes the marriage impossible are argued under it. Whether a given reason is “serious” is decided on the facts of each case.

→How You End It, Even From Abroad

Rescission is made by a declaration to the other party (Art. 805). It does not need a court. If you cannot reach the other person at all, the engagement is treated as rescinded when you learned of the ground for rescission.

When one partner has gone back to their home country, the declaration is usually a message or e-mail. Keep it, keep the reply, and keep what led up to it. Those messages are often the main evidence later on the question that matters most: whose fault it was.

→Damages: What the Party at Fault Owes (Art. 806)

When an engagement is rescinded, a party can claim damages from the other party if that party was at fault (Art. 806(1)). The claim covers property loss and also emotional distress (Art. 806(2)) — the Korean term for the second part is 위자료.

  • Property loss — money actually spent because of the engagement and lost because it ended, such as wedding preparations. Receipts and transfer records decide the amount.
  • Emotional distress — a sum the court fixes looking at the whole history. There is no table; do not trust anyone who quotes a fixed figure.
  • Personal claim — the claim for emotional distress cannot be transferred or inherited unless an agreement on it was already made or a lawsuit already filed (Art. 806(3)).

The party who ended the engagement is not automatically the one at fault. If you broke it off because of what the other person did, Article 804 is your answer to their claim — and the basis of your own.

→The Rings and Gifts (예물)

The Supreme Court treats engagement gifts as similar to a gift made on the condition that the marriage takes place — if the marriage does not happen, the gift is in principle returned. Two rulings shape the rest:

  • The party at fault cannot demand back what they gave (Supreme Court 76므41, 1976-12-28).
  • Once the marriage took place and lasted a considerable time, the gifts are not returned even if the couple later divorces, and even if the recipient caused the breakdown, unless special circumstances exist — for example, the recipient never intended to stay married in good faith (Supreme Court 96다5506, 1996-05-14).

So the gift question turns on the same point as damages: who was at fault for the engagement ending.

→Where the Case Goes: Family Court, Mediation First

Claims for damages and restoration arising from rescission of an engagement — including claims against a third party, such as a parent who broke it up — belong to the Family Court (가정법원) exclusively (Family Litigation Act, 가사소송법, Art. 2(1)). They are 다류 family cases, and for those you must first apply for mediation (조정) before suing (Art. 50). Many engagement disputes end in mediation with an agreed payment and the gifts returned.

If the other party now lives outside Korea, a case in Korea is still possible, but serving the papers and collecting on a result abroad take time. That is a reason to try a written settlement first.

→What to Do Now

  1. Save the record — messages about the engagement, the break-up and the reasons given.
  2. List what was spent and given, by whom, with receipts and transfers.
  3. Check cancellation terms with the wedding hall and other vendors before more fees accrue.
  4. Make your declaration in writing if you are the one ending it, and state the reason.
  5. Put any settlement in writing — the amount, the gifts and that it closes all claims.
  6. Get advice before suing: mediation comes first, and fault is the whole case.
