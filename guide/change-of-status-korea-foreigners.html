Changing Your Visa Status Inside Korea: The Status Change Permit (체류자격 변경허가) Explained

By Juan, Attorney at Law 8 min read English

Your circumstances changed — a job offer, a marriage, a graduation. Your visa did not change with them. In Korea the status you hold and the activity you are actually doing have to match, and closing that gap is a separate permit with its own deadline.

Foreign residents often describe what they need as "renewing my visa," but Korean immigration treats three completely different applications under that phrase. Getting them confused is one of the most common ways an otherwise lawful stay turns into an overstay.

→Three Permits That Are Not Interchangeable

An extension of stay (체류기간 연장허가) keeps the same status and pushes the expiry date back. A status change permit (체류자격 변경허가) replaces one status with another — a student becomes a worker, a job seeker becomes a specialist. An activity-outside-status permit (체류자격 외 활동허가) leaves your status untouched but allows a specific additional activity, which is how a D-2 student legally works part-time.

1The Application Has to Land Before the Current Status Expires

This is the point that costs people the most. A status change permit (체류자격 변경허가) is not a fresh start — it is a change to a stay that is still valid. If your current status has already lapsed when you apply, you are not a person requesting a change; you are a person who overstayed, and the overstay is dealt with first, usually as a fine and sometimes as a bar on the very change you wanted.

Reservations at an immigration office through the HiKorea system can run weeks out in busy periods, and the reservation date is what counts, not the day you decided to apply. Treat the last month of your current status as the deadline for having an appointment already booked, not for starting to think about it.

2Not Every Change Can Be Made From Inside Korea

Some switches are routinely approved in-country. Others are treated as new entries: the change is refused inside Korea and the applicant is expected to obtain a certificate of visa issuance (사증발급인정서) and enter on the new status from abroad. Short-term statuses in particular are often blocked from converting in-country, precisely because converting them would defeat the purpose of the short-term category.

Whether a specific route is available in-country depends on the pair of statuses involved and on current immigration guidance, which is revised more often than the statute is. Confirming the route before resigning from a job or ending an enrollment is the difference between a paperwork step and an unplanned flight home.

The Routes People Ask About Most

  • D-2 student to E-7 (특정활동) — usually turns on whether the degree and the job description line up, and on the employer meeting its own requirements.
  • D-2 or D-4 to D-10 (구직) — the job seeker bridge, used to stay lawfully while looking for the job that supports a work status.
  • E-9 (비전문취업) to E-7-4 — the skilled worker route, scored on time worked, income, and qualifications rather than granted on request.
  • Any status to F-6 (결혼이민) — a marriage registered in Korea does not change status on its own; the change is a separate application with its own income and housing requirements.

Immigration does not ask what you are entitled to in the abstract. It asks whether the status you hold today can legally become the status you want, from inside the country, on the day you apply.

3What the Office Looks At Beyond the Forms

The document list is the visible part of the application. The discretionary part is your record: previous overstays however brief, criminal dispositions including fines and a summary order (약식명령), unpaid taxes or national health insurance premiums, and any earlier report that turned out to be inaccurate.

None of these is automatically fatal, and many applicants with a blemish are approved. But they change the application from a formality into something that needs explaining, and the explanation works far better when it is prepared in advance than when it is improvised at the counter.

→If the Change Is Refused

  1. Find out what your status is right now — a refusal sometimes comes with a short departure period rather than an immediate loss of status, and the two situations call for different responses.
  2. Get the reason in writing — a verbal explanation at the counter is not something you can build an objection or a second application on.
  3. Distinguish a fixable gap from a categorical bar — a missing document or an employer requirement that can be met is a different problem from a route that is simply not permitted in-country.
  4. Do not let the current status run out while you decide — the clock does not pause because an application was refused, and an overstay narrows every option that follows.
