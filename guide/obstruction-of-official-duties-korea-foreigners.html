Pushed a Police Officer After a Night Out? Obstruction of Official Duties (공무집행방해) in Korea

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

A shove at a taxi rank after a night out. An officer's hand slapped away during an ID check. A threat shouted across the counter of a police box. In Korea, using force or threats against a public official who is doing the job is a crime of its own: obstruction of the performance of official duties (공무집행방해, gongmu jiphaeng banghae). Its maximum is 5 years or 10 million won; the same push against a stranger tops out at 2 years or 5 million won.

These cases often start small: drinks, an argument, someone calls the police, and the person being questioned turns on the officer. The first problem may have been minor. The second one is not, because unlike a scuffle between private people, it does not go away when the officer accepts an apology.

→What Article 136 Punishes

Article 136(1) of the Criminal Act punishes anyone who uses violence or threats against a public official who is performing official duties, with up to 5 years' imprisonment or a fine of up to 10 million won. Article 136(2) gives the same penalty to violence or threats used to force an official to do or not do something, or to make them resign.

  • Police officers are the usual case, not the only one. The article covers any public official on duty: firefighters and 119 ambulance crews, immigration officers, officials at a district office.
  • No injury is needed. The offence is complete with violence or a threat. An injury makes it heavier.
  • Deception counts too. Article 137 punishes obstructing an official's duties by deception (위계) with the same maximum.

→Why “The Officer Forgave Me” Does Not End It

For simple assault (폭행) between private people, Article 260(3) of the Criminal Act bars prosecution against the victim's express wish. That is why a settlement (합의) so often closes those cases. Article 136 has no such clause. The offence protects the work of the State, not the officer as a private person, so the case goes on whatever the officer says.

An apology and compensation still matter. If the officer was hurt or equipment was broken, paying for that loss is something the prosecutor and the court weigh in your favour. It is a mitigating factor, not a way out.

→Drunk Is Not a Defence

Article 10 of the Criminal Act lets a court acquit, or reduce the sentence of, someone whose capacity to judge was absent or impaired. Article 10(3) withdraws that relief from a person who foresaw the danger and brought on that state voluntarily. Heavy drinking followed by a fight with the police is the kind of situation that clause is aimed at, so “I don't remember” is rarely a strategy.

What matters more is where the line falls between a crime and a minor offence. Shouting and behaving very rowdily while drunk at a police station or other public office, without violence or threats against an official, is a minor offence under the Minor Offenses Act (Art. 3(3)(i)): a fine of up to 600,000 won, short detention or a petty fine. Once a hand goes on the officer, or the words become a threat, it is Article 136.

Shouting at the police station is a minor offence. A hand on the officer is a criminal case.

→Arrested on the Spot

A person who is committing a crime, or has just committed one, is a flagrant offender (현행범인) and can be arrested without a warrant (Criminal Procedure Act Arts. 211 and 212). That is why these cases often begin with a night in a police cell rather than a summons. What happens next, and how long you can be held, is in arrest, detention warrants and bail.

The article protects an official who is performing official duties. Whether the officer was in fact doing that at the moment, for example whether a stop or an arrest followed the required procedure, is part of the case, not a side issue. If you think it was not, raise it with a lawyer before your first interview, while the video can still be found.

→If You Are Charged

Since 2 October 2026 the prosecutor at the public prosecution office (공소청) decides whether to indict on the basis of the police file and does not question suspects, so the police interview is the statement that counts.

  1. Do not argue the case in the cell. What you say at the station is noted, and often recorded.
  2. Ask for an interpreter if your Korean is not fluent, and do not sign a statement you have not had read back to you. Your rights are set out in questioned by Korean police.
  3. Find the video early. CCTV from the bar, the street or the taxi, and clips friends filmed. Recordings are overwritten after a while; ask for them, or have a lawyer ask, quickly.
  4. Deal with the officer's loss. Medical costs or damaged equipment can be paid and documented. It will not end the case but it is weighed.
  5. Think about your visa. An open case can lead to a departure ban (출국정지). Under the Immigration Act (Art. 46(1)(xiii)), a foreigner who has been sentenced to imprisonment without labour (금고) or a heavier penalty and released can be deported, and a conviction can weigh on visa renewals. See deportation and entry bans.

The limitation period for prosecuting an Article 136 offence is 7 years (Criminal Procedure Act Art. 249(1)(iv)).
