Work Visa Types in Korea: Which One Fits Your Job, Your Country and Your Plans

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

People ask for “a work visa for Korea” as if there were one. There is not. Korean immigration law grants a status of stay (체류자격) for a defined activity, and the real questions are which activity, for which employer, and from which country you are applying.

This guide is a map, not a full treatment of each visa. Each status below has its own guide, and the details that decide applications (quotas, points tables, salary thresholds) are set by immigration notices that change from time to time. Check the current version before you apply.

→Your Visa Is Permission for One Kind of Work

A Korean status of stay defines what you may do. An E-7 is permission to do a designated professional job for the sponsoring employer. An E-2 is permission to teach a language under a specific arrangement. A dependent on F-3 has no work permission at all by default. Paid work outside that definition needs permission in advance, and doing it without one is an immigration violation in the same family as overstaying. Our guide on activities outside your status covers this in detail.

2Applying From Abroad: Which Doors Are Actually Open

For someone outside Korea with no Korean ancestry and no Korean spouse, the realistic routes are few:

  • A job offer in an E-7 category. The job title is not enough: the duties, your qualifications and the sponsoring company all have to fit. See the E-7 guide.
  • E-2 language teaching, if you are a native speaker of the language and clear the document checks (degree, apostilled criminal record check, medical check). See the E-2 guide.
  • A working holiday (H-1), only if your country has a working holiday agreement with Korea. Age limits, length of stay and quotas come from each agreement, so check with the Korean embassy or consulate in your country.
  • E-9 through the Employment Permit System, only if your country takes part in it. You do not apply to an employer directly; you are selected and matched through the system. See the E-9 guide.
  • Study first. A degree from a Korean university leads many people to D-10 and then to E-7. Students may work only part-time and only with a permit; see the D-2 work permit guide.

The question is never just “can I get a work visa?” It is “which status, for which job, sponsored by whom?”

3Already in Korea: Moving Between Statuses

Many people reach the status they want in steps. Graduates of Korean universities often move from D-2 to D-10 while job hunting, then to E-7 once an employer sponsors them; see the D-10 guide. Workers on E-9 or E-10 can sometimes move into the skilled worker category commonly called E-7-4, decided by a points system with an annual quota. Residents who score enough on the F-2-7 points table can leave employer sponsorship behind altogether; see the F-2-7 guide. Each of these is an application, not a formality: see changing your visa status inside Korea.

4The Mistakes That Cost People Their Status

  • Starting a new job before the change is approved. On E-7, E-2 and E-9 the new workplace has to be approved first. Working there before that is unauthorized employment, even with a signed contract.
  • A second job “in the same field”. Permission is tied to the workplace as well as the activity, so a second employer usually needs its own permission.
  • Working on D-10 or F-3. Neither status allows paid work without prior permission.
  • A contract salary that is not the real salary. Immigration compares the declared salary with tax and pension records, and a mismatch can surface at renewal.

The employer is exposed too: hiring someone for work their status does not allow is itself a violation. That is why a careful employer asks to see your permission before your first day.

→Before You Accept a Job Offer

  1. Ask which status the employer will sponsor, and which category within it.
  2. Check that your qualifications match that category's requirements, not just the job title.
  3. If you are already in Korea, find out whether you need a change of status or a change of workplace, and do not start work until it is approved.
  4. Applying from abroad? Ask the Korean embassy whether your country has a working holiday agreement or takes part in the Employment Permit System.
  5. Make sure the contract states the real salary.
