Apostille and Document Authentication (아포스티유) in Korea: Getting Papers Recognized Abroad

By Juan, Attorney at Law 6 min read English

A Korean government document, on its own, means nothing to a foreign registry office or embassy. Something has to certify that it's genuine — and which certificate you need depends entirely on where it's going.

Whether you're registering a Korean marriage abroad, using a Korean degree for a foreign job application, or submitting a criminal background check (범죄경력회보서) to another country's immigration authority, the receiving country generally won't accept the document at face value. Korea has been a member of the Hague Apostille Convention since 2007, which means most foreign-bound documents can be authenticated with a single certificate — an apostille (아포스티유) — instead of the older, slower route through an embassy or consulate. The catch is knowing which office issues it, and when apostille doesn't apply at all.

→Apostille vs. Consular Legalization — Not the Same Process

An apostille (아포스티유) works only between countries that are both parties to the Hague Apostille Convention. If the document is headed to a country that isn't a member, apostille isn't available for that destination, and the document instead needs consular legalization (영사확인) — a longer process that generally involves authentication by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (외교부) followed by confirmation from that country's embassy or consulate in Korea. Sending a document to the wrong process is one of the most common ways people lose weeks: check the destination country's Hague Convention membership status before choosing which certificate to request.

→Two Different Agencies Issue Apostilles, Depending on the Document

Most administrative documents — family relation certificates (가족관계증명서), certificates of citizenship, business registration certificates, and similar records issued by government agencies — are apostilled through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (외교부), either online through the apostille portal or in person. Documents that are notarial in nature, such as a notarized power of attorney or a document certified by a Korean notary public, generally go through the Ministry of Justice / court administration channel (법원행정처 or a notary office authorized for apostille issuance) instead. Sending a notarized document to the wrong office, or vice versa, is a common reason applications bounce back.

Diplomas and Academic Records

University diplomas and transcripts intended for use abroad typically need to be confirmed by the issuing school first (sometimes through a separate authentication by the Ministry of Education) before an apostille can be attached, particularly if the receiving institution or employer requires the academic record itself to be pre-verified. Confirm your specific school's procedure before assuming the diploma alone is apostille-ready.

1Common Situations Where This Comes Up

Foreign residents in Korea most often need an apostille when registering a Korean marriage with a home-country registry, applying for a visa or residency abroad based on Korean-issued documents, using a Korean criminal background check (범죄경력회보서) for immigration or employment overseas, or when a Korean company document needs to be recognized by a foreign business partner or court. Each of these can require slightly different supporting documents alongside the apostille itself, so it's worth confirming the receiving institution's exact requirements before applying, not after.

Apostille only works if the destination country is on the Hague Convention list. If it isn't, you need a completely different process — not a faster version of the same one.

2Translation Is a Separate Question From Authentication

An apostille certifies that the underlying Korean document and the signature/seal on it are genuine — it does not translate anything. If the receiving country requires the document in its own language, a certified translation is typically needed in addition to the apostille, and some countries require the translation itself to be apostilled or notarized separately. Assuming the apostilled Korean-language original will simply be accepted abroad is a frequent and avoidable mistake.

→What to Actually Do Before You Apply

  1. Confirm the destination country is a Hague Convention member before choosing apostille over consular legalization — they are not interchangeable.
  2. Identify the right issuing agency for your specific document type — administrative documents and notarial documents often go through different channels.
  3. Ask the receiving institution what else they require alongside the apostille — translation, additional certified copies, or a specific document format.
  4. Don't assume translation is included — budget separate time for a certified translation if the destination requires the document in another language.
