A Korean Police Case After You Have Left Korea: What Happens to It, and How to Handle It From Abroad

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

You finished your contract, flew home, and now a Korean investigator is sending you messages. Or you left in a hurry after an argument that ended at a police box. Either way, the file in Korea did not close when your plane took off. What happens next depends on what you do from where you are.

This guide is for the person who is outside Korea with a criminal case still open there — as a suspect, not as a victim or witness.

→Leaving Does Not Close the Case

Since 2 October 2026 the police investigate and the prosecutor at the public prosecution office (공소청) decides, on the file the police send, whether to charge. The police question suspects; prosecutors no longer do.

The police may require a suspect to attend and give a statement when the investigation needs it (Criminal Procedure Act, 형사소송법, Art. 200). That summons usually arrives as a phone call, a text or a letter. If there are reasonable grounds to suspect you and you fail to answer a summons without good reason — or are likely to — the police can apply, through the prosecutor, for an arrest warrant from a judge (Art. 200-2(1)).

A case whose suspect cannot be reached is typically put on hold, not closed. It stays on the system until you come back or are found.

→The Clock Can Stop While You Are Away

Every offence has a limitation period for prosecution (공소시효). Many people assume that staying away long enough will run it out. Article 253(3) of the Criminal Procedure Act closes that route:

If the offender is outside Korea for the purpose of escaping criminal punishment, the limitation period is suspended for that time.

Whether you left “to escape punishment” is a question of fact. A departure planned long before anything happened looks different from one booked the day after a police summons, and staying away after you know about the case can count too. Do not build a plan on the hope that the clock is running. See limitation periods in Korea.

→If the Case Goes to Court Without You

  • Summary order (약식명령). For many minor offences the prosecutor asks for a fine on the papers, and the court can impose it with no hearing (Art. 448). You have seven days from service to request a formal trial (Art. 453). If the order reaches an old address and the days pass, the fine becomes a final conviction, and an unpaid criminal fine can be converted into detention in a workhouse (노역장 유치). See summary orders.
  • Trial. As a rule a court cannot hold a hearing if the defendant is absent (Art. 276). There is an exception: under Article 23 of the Act on Special Cases Concerning Expedition of Legal Proceedings (소송촉진 등에 관한 특례법), if the defendant cannot be located for six months after the court reports that service failed, the first-instance court can try the case without the defendant’s statement. It does not apply to offences punishable by death, life imprisonment, or more than 10 years, subject to exceptions the Act lists.

Being abroad can therefore end in a conviction you only learn about later.

→Coming Back to Korea

If a warrant is outstanding, you can be arrested when you arrive. What follows — questioning, and possibly a detention-warrant hearing — is in arrest and the detention warrant hearing. If you are not arrested but the case is still open, you may be stopped from leaving again until it is dealt with: see departure bans.

A planned return is far better than a surprise one. Agreeing a date with the investigator in advance, through a lawyer, shows you are not avoiding the case — which is exactly what a warrant application has to show.

→What Can Be Done From Abroad

  • Find out what the case is. The offence, the stage, and whether a warrant has been issued. A Korean defence lawyer can contact the investigator for you.
  • Appoint a lawyer without travelling. The appointment form is signed by you and sent from abroad. For other documents, see power of attorney from abroad.
  • Put your side on the file. A written statement and evidence can go to the police before any interview.
  • Settlement with the victim. In many cases a settlement weighs heavily in the outcome, and in some offences it ends the case. It can be negotiated through a lawyer. See settlement agreements.
  • Plan the interview. If you must attend in person, fix a date and prepare before you fly.

→What to Do Now

  1. Do not ignore the messages. Silence is what turns a summons into a warrant.
  2. Do not explain the facts by text or phone to the investigator before you have advice — it can be recorded as a statement.
  3. Write down the investigator’s name, the police station, the case number and every date you were contacted.
  4. Check your old Korean address for court mail, because the seven-day deadline runs from service.
  5. Talk to a Korean lawyer before you book a flight to Korea.
