Suing the Person Your Spouse Had an Affair With in Korea: 상간자 위자료, Deadlines and Evidence

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

Adultery stopped being a crime in Korea in 2016. It did not stop being a legal wrong. A person who has an affair with someone they know is married can be sued for damages for emotional harm (위자료, wijaryo) by the betrayed spouse, whether or not that spouse goes on to divorce. Koreans call this a 상간자 소송 (sanggan-ja sosong). There is a deadline, and there is one situation in which the claim fails.

Foreign spouses come to this question from both sides: as the spouse who found the messages, or as the person who has just received a complaint from a Korean court naming them as the third party. This guide covers the legal basis, when the claim does not work, the deadlines, the court, evidence, and what changes when someone involved lives abroad.

→No Longer a Crime, Still a Civil Wrong

The old crime of adultery (간통죄, Criminal Act Art. 241) was struck down by the Constitutional Court on 26 February 2015 and deleted from the Criminal Act on 6 January 2016. Nobody can be prosecuted for an affair as such, and reporting one to the police leads nowhere.

The civil side is a different matter. In an en banc judgment of 20 November 2014 (2011므2997), the Supreme Court held that a third party who has an affair with a married person, and in doing so interferes with the couple's shared married life and infringes the other spouse's rights as a spouse, commits a tort in principle. The general tort rules then apply: liability for harm caused intentionally or negligently (Civil Act Art. 750), including non-financial harm such as mental suffering (Art. 751).

  • The third party must have known, or should have known, that the person was married. Article 750 requires intent or negligence, so someone who was genuinely and reasonably deceived about the marriage is in a different position.
  • The unfaithful spouse is liable too. Where two people together cause the harm, they are jointly liable for the whole of it (Art. 760). You can sue the third party alone, the spouse alone, or both.

→When the Claim Fails: a Marriage Already Over

The same 2014 judgment drew the limit. If the couple's married life had already broken down beyond repair, for example after a long separation, a sexual relationship with a third party is not a tort against the other spouse. That holds even if no divorce has been filed, and even while a divorce case is still pending.

This is the defence most third parties raise, and it turns on facts: when the couple stopped living together, what they said to each other, whether they still shared a home, money or family life. A spouse who moved out last month is not in the same position as a couple who have lived apart for years.

The question is not whether you are still married on paper. It is whether there was still a marriage to damage when the affair began.

→The Deadlines

ClaimTime limitSource
Damages (위자료) from the third party or the spouse3 years from knowing of the harm and who caused it; 10 years from the act at the latestCivil Act Art. 766
Divorce on the ground of infidelity6 months from knowing; 2 years from the act. Lost if you consented in advance or forgave afterwardsCivil Act Arts. 840(i), 841

The two clocks are separate. The six-month and forgiveness rules in Article 841 are written for the divorce claim on this ground; other divorce grounds may still be open, and a damages claim follows Article 766. If you have taken your spouse back, get advice on how that affects each claim before filing.

→Which Court

  • Together with a divorce: the Family Litigation Act treats a damages claim caused by the divorce, including a claim against a third party, as a family court case (Art. 2(1)(i)(c)(2)). It can be heard with the divorce, and mediation comes first (Art. 50).
  • Without a divorce: a claim against the third party alone is normally filed as an ordinary civil damages suit in the district court.

Korean courts set the amount of 위자료 themselves; there is no table. The facts usually argued over include how long the marriage had lasted, how long and how openly the affair went on, whether it is what ended the marriage, whether there are children, and how each side behaved once it came out.

→Evidence, and What Not to Do

You need to show three things: the relationship, that the third party knew about the marriage, and that the marriage was still a functioning one at the time. Messages, photographs, hotel or card records, and what was said after the discovery are the usual material.

  • Be careful how you get it. Recording conversations you are not part of, logging into your spouse's accounts or installing tracking apps can be offences in their own right. See recording conversations in Korea.
  • Do not go public. Posting about the affair, messaging the third party's employer or family, or turning up at their home can lead to a defamation or threats complaint against you. See defamation in Korea.
  • If you are the one being sued, do not ignore the papers. A defendant who does not answer a civil complaint in time can lose without a hearing. See being sued in Korea.

→When Someone Lives Abroad

Jurisdiction. Korean courts can hear a claim against a person whose habitual residence is in Korea (Private International Law Act Art. 3), and more generally where the dispute has a substantial connection with Korea (Art. 2). Suing a third party who has left Korea is possible in some cases, but slower, and a judgment is harder to enforce.

Which law applies. A tort is governed by the law of the place where the act was done or the result occurred, but if both sides habitually lived in the same country at the time, that country's law applies (Art. 52(1)-(2)). An affair in Korea between residents of Korea will normally be judged under Korean law. Many countries do not allow this kind of claim at all, so where the facts point abroad, the applicable law can decide the case.

→What to Do Now

  1. Write down the date you found out. The three years for damages, and the six months for a divorce on this ground, run from that knowledge.
  2. Keep what you already have lawfully: screenshots with dates, the other person's name and contact details, and anything showing they knew you were married.
  3. Note the state of the marriage before and after: where each of you lived, shared accounts, family events. This is what the already-broken defence will be argued on.
  4. Decide whether you are divorcing. It changes the court and whether the claims can be combined. See divorce in Korea as a foreigner and property division and 위자료.
  5. If you hold a marriage-based visa, think about your status at the same time: keeping an F-6 visa after the marriage ends.
