Scammed on a Secondhand Marketplace in Korea (중고거래 사기): Evidence, the Police Report and Getting the Money Back

By Juan, Attorney at Law 8 min read English · Español · Português

Secondhand apps are how most foreigners in Korea furnish an apartment, sell a bicycle before leaving, or buy concert tickets. They are also where the most ordinary fraud in the country happens — and the rules for getting money back are not the ones people expect.

The pattern rarely varies. A listing for a laptop, a camera or a pair of tickets, priced a little below the market. A friendly seller who prefers to move the conversation to KakaoTalk, explains that they cannot meet in person, and asks for a bank transfer — or sends a "safe payment" link that looks like a real platform page. The money goes. The parcel never arrives. The account stops answering.

Secondhand-trade fraud (중고거래 사기) is ordinary fraud under the Criminal Act (형법, Article 347), punishable by up to ten years' imprisonment. It is investigated seriously, particularly where one account has taken money from many people. But what a victim does in the first days decides most of what can be recovered.

→The First 24 Hours

  • Stop paying. Scammers often come back with a second request — a "delivery fee," a "deposit to confirm," a refund that requires you to send money first. No legitimate refund requires a payment from you
  • Preserve everything before it disappears. Screenshots of the listing, the seller's profile, the whole chat including the parts on other apps, the account number and name, the transfer receipt, and any links you were sent. Listings and accounts are deleted quickly, and the chat history is your main evidence
  • Note the platform's internal identifiers. The seller's nickname, user ID and listing number help the police obtain records from the platform
  • Report to the platform. It blocks the account and preserves its own records

A screenshot taken today is evidence. A listing you meant to capture tomorrow is gone.

→Why Your Bank Cannot Simply Freeze the Account

Victims of voice phishing in Korea can ask their bank to freeze the receiving account within minutes, under a special statute for telecommunications financial fraud (통신사기피해환급법). Many secondhand-scam victims assume the same procedure applies to them. In general, it does not: that statute expressly excludes fraud that pretends to supply goods or services, which is what a fake sale is.

That leaves two practical routes to stopping the money. The first is the criminal investigation, which can identify the account holder and lead to the seizure of proceeds. The second is a civil preservation measure — a provisional attachment (가압류) of the receiving account, which you apply for yourself and which requires identifying the account holder. Neither is as fast as a bank freeze, which is why speed in reporting matters.

→Reporting to the Police

You can report at any police station or through the police's online cybercrime reporting system. Interpretation is available, and you do not need to speak Korean to file a complaint. Bring, or upload:

  1. Your passport or residence card
  2. The transfer record showing the date, amount and receiving account
  3. The screenshots of the listing, profile and conversation
  4. A short written timeline in your own words

When several victims have paid the same account, the cases are usually combined. Search the account number and phone number in private fraud-report databases such as The Cheat (더치트): finding other victims both confirms the pattern and strengthens the case.

Expect the investigation to take months rather than weeks. Scams are often run through accounts borrowed or bought from other people (대포통장), and the person whose name is on the account is not always the person who took the money — though lending an account for this purpose is itself a crime in Korea.

→Getting the Money Back

A conviction does not by itself return your money. There are three routes, and they can be combined:

  • Settlement during the case. Defendants facing a fraud charge often repay victims to obtain a lighter sentence. If the police or prosecutor contacts you about a settlement, you are entitled to insist on payment before signing anything that says you do not want the defendant punished
  • A compensation order (배상명령). In the criminal trial, the victim can ask the court to order the defendant to pay the amount lost. It costs nothing, needs no separate lawsuit, and the order can be enforced like a judgment
  • A civil claim. For the amounts typical of secondhand fraud, a payment order (지급명령) or small claims case (소액사건) is quick and inexpensive. Where you know only an account number, the court can be asked to obtain the account holder's details from the bank

→When the Foreigner Is the One Accused

There is a second way foreigners get caught up in these cases, and it is worth knowing. In "triangle fraud" (삼자사기), a scammer pretends to buy your item and, at the same time, pretends to sell a different item to a third person — giving that person your account number. The third person pays you; you ship your item to the scammer; the third person, who received nothing, reports your account.

You did nothing wrong, but your account may be reported as a fraud account, restricted, and your name may appear in a police complaint. Keep your own records of every sale — the listing, the chat with the buyer and the shipping receipt — so you can show that you were also a victim. If your account is restricted, or the police contact you, respond early and with the evidence, rather than waiting for the matter to escalate.

→Habits That Prevent Most of This

  • Meet in person for anything of value, and check the item before paying
  • Use the platform's own payment system, opened from inside the app — never a link sent in a chat
  • Be wary of any seller who moves the conversation off the platform
  • Never send a photo of your passport or residence card to a stranger; it can be used to open accounts in your name
