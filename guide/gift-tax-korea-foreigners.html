Gift Tax (증여세) in Korea for Foreign Residents: What Counts as a Taxable Gift

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

Your parents wired money to help with a deposit. Your Korean spouse put the apartment in your name. Neither of you called it a "gift" — but Korea's National Tax Service might.

Foreign residents in Korea often assume gift tax (증여세) is something that only applies to wealthy families passing down businesses. In practice, it reaches far more ordinary situations: a wire transfer from parents abroad to help with rent, a spouse covering a jeonse deposit in the other spouse's name, or a piece of property registered under a family member instead of the person who actually paid for it. Any transfer of money or property without adequate payment in return can be treated as a gift, and the recipient — not the giver — is generally the one who owes the tax in Korea.

→Whether You're Taxed on Worldwide or Korea-Only Gifts Depends on Residency

Korea's gift tax follows the same resident/non-resident (거주자/비거주자) line that governs income tax. A recipient who is a Korean tax resident is generally taxed on gifted assets regardless of where in the world they're located — including money received into a foreign bank account from a foreign-based relative. A non-resident recipient is generally only taxed on gifts of property located in Korea, such as Korean real estate or a Korean bank deposit. The giver's nationality or residency doesn't decide this; the recipient's status does.

→The Relationship-Based Deductions Often Don't Apply to Foreign Recipients

Korean gift tax law allows deductions based on the relationship between giver and recipient — a set amount every ten years for gifts between spouses, a smaller set amount for gifts from parents to an adult child, and so on. The trap for foreign families is that these standard deductions generally require the recipient to be a Korean tax resident at the time of the gift. A foreign parent living abroad who sends money to a foreign-national child who is themselves a non-resident of Korea may find the usual family deductions don't apply the way they would between two Korean residents, meaning a transfer that would be tax-free between two domestic family members can be fully taxable in a cross-border version of the same gift.

An International Wire Transfer Can Trigger an Automatic Review

Korean banks report incoming foreign remittances above certain thresholds to financial authorities, and the National Tax Service cross-references large or repeated transfers against gift tax filings. A foreign spouse or student who quietly receives support from family abroad for years without ever filing a gift tax return can end up facing an audit years later — with penalties and interest added on top of the original tax, calculated from the date the money actually arrived, not the date the problem was noticed.

"It was just family helping out" is not a legal exemption — it's the exact fact pattern the gift tax filing requirement was written to cover.

1Putting Property in a Family Member's Name Is a Gift, Even Without Cash Changing Hands

Registering an apartment, a car, or investment holdings under a spouse's or child's name — while someone else actually paid for it — is treated as a gift to the person on the title, not a mere convenience. This comes up often with jointly-funded real estate purchases where only one spouse's name goes on the deed for visa, credit, or simplicity reasons. The unnamed contributor's share of the purchase price can be assessed as a gift to the named owner.

2The Filing Deadline Is Short, and It's on the Recipient

A gift tax return must generally be filed within three months from the end of the month in which the gift was received. This deadline applies whether or not the recipient realizes a return is required, and voluntarily filing late — even before an audit starts — generally still carries a reduced penalty rather than none at all. Waiting until the National Tax Service raises the question first is the more expensive outcome.

→What to Actually Do Before Money or Property Changes Hands

  1. Check the recipient's tax residency status first — it decides both the scope of taxation and which deductions are even available.
  2. Don't assume family transfers are automatically exempt — confirm whether the specific relationship and residency combination actually qualifies for a deduction.
  3. Match property titles to who actually paid — putting an asset in a family member's name without accounting for it can itself be a taxable gift.
  4. File within three months if a gift has already happened — a voluntary late filing is treated far better than one prompted by an audit.
