Bought It Cheap, Kept It for a Friend: Handling Stolen Goods (장물죄) in Korea

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

A phone sold far below its price by someone who cannot show a box or a receipt. A friend asks you to keep a bag for a week. An acquaintance asks you to ship some parcels home for him. In Korea, taking part in what happens to property after a theft or a fraud is a crime of its own: handling stolen goods (장물, jangmul).

The person who stole the item is charged with theft. The person who later buys it, keeps it, carries it, sells it on or arranges the sale can be charged under a separate article, even with no part in the theft itself. What decides most of these cases is a single question: what did you know when you took the item?

→What the Criminal Act Punishes

Article 362(1) of the Criminal Act punishes anyone who acquires (취득), transfers (양도), transports (운반) or keeps (보관) stolen goods, with up to 7 years' imprisonment or a fine of up to 15 million won. Article 362(2) gives the same penalty to anyone who arranges (알선) one of those acts, such as finding a buyer for a friend.

  • "Stolen goods" is wider than theft. 장물 means property obtained through a property crime: theft and robbery, but also fraud, extortion and embezzlement. A laptop someone obtained by fraud is 장물 in the same way as a stolen one.
  • The handler is someone else. The thief who sells his own loot is dealt with under the original crime. This article is aimed at the people who come after.
  • Paying is not required. Receiving an item as a gift is still acquiring it, and holding it for someone is keeping it.

→What You Knew Is the Whole Case

Article 362 requires that you knew the item was stolen goods. Korean courts do not require certainty: it is enough that you realized the item might well be stolen and took it anyway. Nobody can see inside your head, so investigators work from the circumstances. The questions they usually ask are these:

  • How far below the normal price was it?
  • Was there a box, a receipt, a warranty card, or a reason the seller could not give one?
  • Did the seller give a name, meet in the open, accept a bank transfer, or insist on cash at night?
  • Were there several identical new items, or was the phone still locked to someone else's account?
  • What did the two of you say in your messages?

The same goes for keeping and carrying. "My friend asked me to hold a bag" is a normal favor; holding a bag of brand-new electronics with the tags on, for a friend who told you not to ask, is the kind of fact that turns into a charge.

If you find out only later, buying in good faith was not the crime. But once you know, do not sell the item on or keep holding it for the person who gave it to you: Korean courts have treated carrying on after learning the truth as transferring or keeping stolen goods. Stop and get advice on how to hand it over.

The question is not whether the item was stolen. It is what you knew, or must have suspected, when you took it.

→Shops and Dealers: Negligence Is Enough

Article 364 reaches people who did not know but should have. Anyone who takes stolen goods through occupational negligence or gross negligence faces up to 1 year's imprisonment without labor (금고) or a fine of up to 5 million won. This is the article that matters if you run or work at a pawnshop, a used-phone shop, a secondhand store or a business buying gold or electronics: the law expects someone who buys these goods for a living to check who is selling and why.

For a dealer, the usual protection is routine: check and record the seller's identification, keep the transaction record, and walk away from deals that do not add up. A file that shows those checks were made is the best answer to a negligence charge.

→When Family Is Involved

Article 365 was rewritten on 31 December 2025, following the 2024 Constitutional Court decision on the family-property rule:

  • If you are a relative of the victim, Article 328 applies: you can be prosecuted only if the victim files a complaint (고소).
  • If you are the spouse, a lineal blood relative or a cohabiting relative of the person who stole the item (or the spouse of one of them), the court may reduce or remit your sentence. This does not extend to accomplices who lack that relationship.

→If the Police Contact You

Since 2 October 2026 the prosecutor at the public prosecution office (공소청) decides whether to indict on the basis of the police file and does not question suspects, so the police interview is the statement that counts.

  1. Do not contact the seller to agree on a story. It looks like coordination, and the messages end up in the file.
  2. Keep and bring your own record: the listing or advertisement, every message with the seller, the transfer receipt and the account it went to. If the price and the conversation looked like an ordinary deal, these are what show it.
  3. Expect the item to be seized as evidence. Arguing over the object at the station does not help your case.
  4. Prepare before the interview. Know which act you are accused of (acquiring, keeping, transporting) and give one consistent account. Your rights are set out in questioned by Korean police.
  5. Think about your visa. An open case can lead to a departure ban (출국정지), and a conviction can affect renewals or lead to deportation proceedings.

The limitation period for prosecution follows the maximum penalty: 7 years for Article 362, 10 years for the habitual offence, and 5 years for the negligence offence (Criminal Procedure Act Art. 249(1)).

→Who Keeps the Item, and Getting Your Money Back

Even if you are not charged, you may lose the item. Under the Civil Act, someone who buys a movable thing peacefully, openly, in good faith and without negligence normally becomes its owner (Art. 249). For stolen or lost items there is an exception: the victim can demand the item back within 2 years of the theft or loss (Art. 250; this does not apply to money).

  • Bought from a private person, for example on a secondhand app: the victim can take the item back without paying you anything. Your claim for the price is against the person who sold it to you.
  • Bought at an auction, a public market, or from a merchant who sells that kind of goods, in good faith: the victim has to reimburse what you paid in order to get the item back (Art. 251).

If the seller vanished with your money, the steps for tracing them and reporting it are in scammed on a secondhand marketplace.
