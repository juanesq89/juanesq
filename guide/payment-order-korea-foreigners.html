Payment Order in Korea (지급명령): How to Collect a Debt Without a Hearing

By Juan, Attorney at Law 7 min read English · Español · Português

A client who never paid the last invoice, a landlord who keeps the deposit, a loan that was never repaid. When the amount is clear and the other side simply is not paying, Korean law offers a procedure with no hearing at all: the payment order (지급명령, jigeup myeongnyeong).

Most people who are owed money in Korea think of two options: a demand letter or a lawsuit. There is a third, set out in Part V of the Civil Procedure Act (민사소송법) as the demand procedure (독촉절차). The court looks only at your application, orders the debtor to pay, and if the debtor does not object in time, that order carries the same weight as a final judgment. It suits an undisputed debt. It suits a debtor who will fight, or who cannot be reached in Korea, much less well.

This guide is written for the creditor. If you have received a payment order, read what to do when you are sued in Korea instead: the two-week deadline below runs against you.

→What a Payment Order (지급명령) Is

Under Article 462 of the Civil Procedure Act, a creditor can ask the court for a payment order on a claim for money (or for a fixed quantity of other fungible things or securities). Two features set it apart from a lawsuit:

  • No hearing. The order is issued without hearing the debtor (Art. 467). The court decides on your application; nobody has to come to court.
  • The debtor must be reachable in Korea. Article 462 limits the procedure to cases where the order can be served in Korea by a method other than service by public notice (공시송달). A debtor who has left Korea, or whose whereabouts are unknown, cannot be reached this way.

The court can still refuse. An application that falls outside these conditions, is filed with the wrong court, or plainly shows no valid basis for the claim is dismissed, and that dismissal cannot be appealed (Art. 465). You remain free to file a lawsuit afterwards.

→The Two-Week Rule That Decides Everything

The payment order must tell the debtor that they may object (이의신청) within two weeks from the day it was served on them (Art. 468). What happens next depends on that window:

What the debtor doesResult
Nothing for two weeks after serviceThe order has the same effect as a final judgment (Art. 474)
Objects within two weeksThe order loses its effect to the extent of the objection, and the case becomes an ordinary lawsuit (Arts. 470, 472)
Objects, then withdraws, or the objection is dismissed as improperSame as no objection: the order becomes final (Art. 474)

The two weeks are a peremptory period (불변기간) under Article 470(2), so the court cannot simply extend them. Once the order is final, you can enforce it directly on the certified copy (정본) of the order, without the separate certificate of enforceability (집행문) a judgment normally needs (Civil Execution Act, 민사집행법, Art. 58(1)). The exceptions are an order with conditions attached and enforcement by or against a successor of a party.

Silence is the debtor's choice. Two weeks without an objection and the order stands like a final judgment.

→If the Debtor Objects, Your Filing Date Is Kept

Creditors sometimes hesitate because they expect an objection. The law protects the date. When the debtor objects properly, a lawsuit is deemed filed on the day you applied for the payment order, for the amount objected to (Art. 472(2)). The same applies if the court orders you to correct the debtor's address and you ask to switch to a lawsuit instead (Arts. 466(1), 472(1)).

What you must do is pay the difference in court stamps. The court sets a period and orders you to top up the stamp duty from the payment-order amount to the full amount for a complaint (Art. 473(1)). If you do not pay within that period, the application is dismissed (Art. 473(2)). Once the stamps are paid, the file goes to the court that will hear the case, and the costs of the payment-order stage become part of the litigation costs (Art. 473(3)–(4)).

Because the date is kept, starting with a payment order does not put you behind on a limitation deadline compared with suing straight away. What it can cost is the weeks spent on service and the objection period, if the debtor was always going to contest the claim.

→What It Costs

The court stamp duty (인지대) for a payment order application is one-tenth of the stamp duty on a complaint for the same amount (Act on Stamps Attached in Civil Litigation, 민사소송 등 인지법, Art. 7(2)). Filed electronically, it is nine-tenths of that figure (Art. 16).

For a claim of 10,000,000 won, the stamp on a complaint would be 50,000 won (Art. 2(1)2: 0.45% plus 5,000 won). A payment order for the same claim therefore needs 5,000 won in stamps, or 4,500 won if filed electronically. Service fees (송달료) for serving the order are paid in advance on top of the stamps.

→When a Payment Order Is the Wrong Tool

  • The debtor has left Korea. If the order could only be served abroad or by public notice, the court may send the case into ordinary litigation on its own decision (Art. 466(2)), and that decision cannot be appealed (Art. 466(3)). Plan for a lawsuit from the start.
  • You have no usable address. If the court orders you to correct the debtor's address, you can supply a new one or ask to convert the case into a lawsuit (Art. 466(1)).
  • The facts are genuinely disputed. A debtor who denies the contract or the amount will object, and you end up in a lawsuit anyway; for 30 million won or less, usually small claims court.
  • The money may disappear first. A payment order freezes nothing. If the debtor could move or spend assets, consider provisional attachment (가압류) before or alongside the application.

One more limit. A final payment order is enforceable, but it does not settle the dispute as firmly as a judgment after trial. The rule that restricts a debtor's later objections to grounds arising after the close of argument (Civil Execution Act Art. 44(2)) does not apply to payment orders (Art. 58(3)), so the debtor can still bring a suit objecting to the claim (청구이의의 소) on grounds that existed earlier.

→Applying as a Foreigner, Step by Step

  1. Gather proof of the debt: the contract or invoice, bank transfer records, and messages in which the debtor acknowledges the amount.
  2. Identify the debtor exactly: full name and a Korean address where they can be served; for a company, its registered name and head office. This is the step that most often stalls the procedure.
  3. Pick the right court. Jurisdiction is exclusive: the district court for the debtor's general forum (usually their address), or one of the other courts the Act lists, such as the court for the place where the obligation was to be performed (Art. 463).
  4. File on paper or electronically. You can file at the court or through the courts' electronic litigation system (전자소송). The rules for a complaint apply where they fit (Art. 464): the parties, the amount claimed and the basis for it.
  5. Wait for service. The order is served on both sides (Art. 469(1)). Count the two weeks from the day the debtor received it. If you live outside Korea, give the court a Korean address for service or act through a Korean attorney.
  6. Enforce. With no objection, obtain the certified copy (정본) of the final order and move to enforcement: bank account seizure, wage garnishment, an asset disclosure order.

→Before You File: A Short Checklist

  • Is the claim for a fixed sum of money, and can you prove it on paper?
  • Do you have an address in Korea where the debtor will actually receive mail?
  • Is the debtor likely to object? If so, weigh going straight to small claims or a lawsuit.
  • Is there a risk the debtor moves money? If so, look at provisional attachment first.
  • Have you checked the limitation period for this kind of claim?

The payment order (지급명령) is the cheapest way to turn an unpaid, undisputed debt into something you can enforce in Korea. It works best when you know where the debtor is and the paperwork speaks for itself; when either is missing, choosing the right procedure first saves months.
